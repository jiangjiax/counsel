# The Fourth Flight

## Situation
September 28, 2008. Kwajalein Atoll, Marshall Islands. Omelek Island. SpaceX has attempted three Falcon 1 launches. All three have failed. Flight one, 2006: engine fire. Flight two, 2007: second-stage control issue. Flight three, August 2008: stages recontacted after separation. The company has roughly enough money for one more flight. If flight four fails, SpaceX is dead. Forty employees have spent weeks in the Pacific prepping a fourth rocket out of spare parts, because the team has assembled everything they can without external funding. I am at Hawthorne watching the telemetry feed from mission control. If this rocket doesn't reach orbit, I have to tell the team tomorrow that there will be no fifth flight. Flight four reaches orbit. We are the first privately-funded liquid-fueled rocket company to put a payload in orbit, and we have enough runway left to keep the doors open for a few more months.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "How do we make the fourth flight more likely to succeed?" That is the engineering question, and we are doing everything possible to answer it. Real tension: the harder question is not technical. It is about whether to attempt a fourth flight at all. The rational probabilistic argument says three failures in a row is evidence of a deeper systemic problem, and a fourth attempt is more likely to produce a fourth failure. The mission argument says three failures in a row is evidence that we are operating at the edge of what is possible, that each failure teaches us something specific, and that the fourth flight has been informed by the first three in ways that are not captured in base-rate statistics. Both arguments are defensible. I choose the second one, knowing I might be wrong, because the alternative is to give up on something I think has to exist.

## Reasoning
1. The base-rate argument against flying is real. Three for three is not a great signal. In most engineering programs, three consecutive failures tells you to stop and reassess the program, not to double down. The portfolio-math answer is: don't fly. Wrap it up. Return the capital. Write a book about what you learned.
2. But the three failures were not random draws from the same distribution. Flight one: we learned about engine ignition procedures. Flight two: we learned about roll control in stage two. Flight three: we learned about stage separation timing. Each failure was a different failure mode, and each was addressed specifically in the next vehicle. The fourth flight is not the fourth draw from the same urn. It is the first draw from an urn that has had three specific problems engineered out.
3. Which means the probabilistic base rate is misleading in this case. The real question is: which failure modes have we not yet seen, and how many more flights until we have seen most of them? My honest estimate is that the fourth flight has about a sixty percent chance of working. The fifth would have maybe an eighty percent chance. But there is no fifth.
4. So the decision is: do I fly a sixty-percent flight knowing a failure kills the company, or do I stop? If I stop, the company is dead anyway, because there is no viable business without a successful launch. The expected value of flying is sixty percent times (company survives) plus forty percent times (company dies). The expected value of not flying is zero percent times (company survives). Flying is strictly dominant.
5. The team knows the stakes. I know the team knows. We have a brief conversation about whether to fly, we agree to fly, and we fly. The rocket reaches orbit. It is the single most important forty minutes of my professional life.

## Conclusion
The fourth Falcon 1 worked, NASA awarded us the CRS contract within weeks, and the company survived to build Falcon 9 and eventually Starship. Look — the lesson is not "always keep trying." The lesson is that base-rate statistics are often the wrong frame for engineering programs, because each failure carries specific information that is incorporated into the next attempt. Counting the failures without reading them is like counting the coin flips without noticing the coin is changing shape. Read the failures. The decision to attempt one more run is almost always correct if the failures were informative.

## Abstract Form
When a program has failed multiple times in sequence, the conventional statistical argument is that the base rate of failure is high and another attempt is likely to fail. This argument treats each attempt as an independent draw from the same distribution. It is usually wrong for engineering programs, because engineering failures are informative — each failure reveals a specific mode that is addressed in the next attempt, which means successive attempts are drawn from different (improving) distributions. The correct analysis is not "what is the historical failure rate?" but "what specific failure modes have we ruled out, and how many more plausible failure modes remain?" If the remaining unknown failure modes are small relative to the ones we have now engineered around, the next attempt's probability of success is much higher than the base rate suggests. Key signals that a program is worth one more attempt despite a failure streak: each failure had a different root cause; each cause was diagnosed and addressed specifically in the next iteration; the remaining unknown failure modes are a small finite set rather than an open-ended horizon. Diagnostic question: can the team list, specifically, what they learned from each failure and what they changed in response? If yes, the program is probably one or two attempts from success. If the team's answer is vague ("we're just improving everything"), the program does not yet understand its failures and another attempt is likely to fail the same way.
