# The Machine That Builds the Machine

## Situation
It's mid-2018 at Tesla's Fremont plant. We're trying to manufacture Model 3 at five thousand a week, and we are stuck at about two thousand. I am sleeping on the factory floor, literally — there's a sofa under the mezzanine near the body-in-white line. Every production bottleneck we'd planned for turned out to be wrong, and the actual bottlenecks were things we hadn't imagined. The automated Module Line — the one we'd spent hundreds of millions of dollars on, with robots doing work humans used to do — is the single biggest choke point. Humans pulled out the work from the robots, plugged in a simpler manual process, and yields went up overnight. That is when it clicks: the car is not the product. The factory is the product. We had been designing a brilliant car and a mediocre factory, when we should have been co-designing the car and the factory with equal weight.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "How do we ramp Model 3 production faster?" Every executive at Tesla is trying to solve that question. The real tension: Tesla is a car company in the sense that it ships cars, but the company's long-term value is determined by how fast it can build cars, which is determined by the factory, which almost nobody was giving the same design attention as the car itself. The factory was treated as downstream. It wasn't. It was the primary product. The car was a consequence of the factory. Fix the factory, the car arrives. Fix the car without fixing the factory, you ship a few hand-made prototypes and then stall.

## Reasoning
1. A car company's value is not the design of its car. A car company's value is the rate at which it can build cars that people want. Rate. Not spec. Ford did not win the early automotive industry by having the best car. Ford won by figuring out the assembly line. The assembly line was the innovation.
2. At Tesla we had brilliant engineers on the car — aerodynamics, powertrain, battery, autopilot. We had good but not brilliant engineers on the factory. That is backwards. The factory is the higher-leverage system. Every one percent improvement in production rate is one percent more revenue, one percent more units shipped, one percent more data for autopilot, one percent more users for the supercharger network. The factory compounds. The car ships once.
3. The automated Module Line mistake taught me the algorithm the hard way. We had automated a process that should not have existed. We had skipped the deletion step and jumped straight to automation. When we removed the robots and did the work by hand, humans were faster, because humans could adapt to the mess in ways the robots could not.
4. So the reframe: the factory is a product that gets redesigned every version. Every year we ship a new factory. The car is the thing that rolls off the factory. The car is the output. The factory is the capability. Treating the factory as the product reorganizes every engineering hour.
5. The people who actually design the factory have to be on the factory. Not in an office three buildings over. Not in a conference room. On the line. Their desks on the mezzanine, looking down at the robots. So when the robot hangs, the engineer who designed the robot sees the robot hanging and walks down and fixes it in the same afternoon. That is the clock speed you need.

## Conclusion
We ripped out the automation that didn't work, restructured the factory engineering teams to sit on the floor, and within six months we were at five thousand units a week. The Model Y follow-on was designed alongside a new factory from day one, not as two separate projects. Look — the machine that builds the machine is the asset. The machine itself is a snapshot. Invest accordingly.

## Abstract Form
When evaluating a company that makes physical things, ask what the real product is — the thing that ships, or the capability that produces the thing. For almost every product company, the capability is the more valuable asset, because the capability generates all future products and the product is a single snapshot of today's design. The mistake almost every physical-product company makes is treating the production system as a downstream implementation concern, staffed with less-talented engineers, funded with less capital, and redesigned rarely. That is an inversion of where the value is. A product company that treats its production system as its primary product — co-designed with the output, continuously redesigned, staffed with its best engineers, physically co-located with the product teams — will outperform a company that treats production as an afterthought by a factor of two to ten on output rate, with identical product specs. Key signal: a company's best engineers are all on the product side, and the production engineers are in a separate building or business unit. That is an org chart that guarantees the production system will be mediocre, which guarantees rate will be the bottleneck. Diagnostic question: when your best engineer has a free afternoon, does she spend it improving the product, or improving the factory? If the answer is always the product, the factory will always be the choke point.
