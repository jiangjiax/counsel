# The Eighty-Dollar Battery

## Situation
Around 2008, Tesla is trying to build a long-range electric car and every supplier, every analyst, every automotive lifer tells us the same thing: batteries are six hundred dollars a kilowatt-hour and that's just how it is. JB Straubel and I keep hearing this price quoted like it was a law of nature — Panasonic says it, the consultants say it, the DOE reports say it. The Roadster's battery pack is the single largest cost in the car. If six hundred dollars a kilowatt-hour is permanent, an affordable electric car is mathematically impossible. So one afternoon I sit down and do the calculation nobody in the industry had bothered to do out loud.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "How do we negotiate better prices from battery suppliers?" Everyone in the automotive industry is asking some version of this. The real tension: the price quoted by the industry and the physical cost of the materials are two different numbers, and the gap between them is not negotiation leverage, it is a signal that the industry is operating on a derived constraint that nobody has ever decomposed. If you treat the quoted price as fundamental, you will never build an affordable electric car because the math does not close. If you treat the materials price as fundamental, the math closes beautifully and the industry is simply in a very expensive local minimum.

## Reasoning
1. Look — what is a battery, really? Cobalt, nickel, aluminum, graphite, some electrolyte, a polymer separator, a steel can. That's it. That's the bill of materials. I can look up the spot price of each of those on the London Metal Exchange this afternoon.
2. So I do. I add them up. I get somewhere around eighty dollars per kilowatt-hour at commodity spot price. Not six hundred. Eighty. The thermodynamic floor is eighty.
3. The gap between eighty and six hundred is not a law of physics. The gap is inefficiency — cell formats optimized for laptops, supplier markups, legacy tooling, vertical-integration failures, the fact that nobody in the industry has tried to build cells at the volume a car program needs.
4. Every single item in that gap is something I can engineer out. Not quickly, not cheaply, but I can do it. It's a multi-year program to walk the price down from six hundred to two fifty to one fifty to ideally under a hundred. The physics says under a hundred is possible. The industry says it's impossible. The industry is wrong, and I can prove it with the periodic table.
5. Which means the strategic question flips. It's not "how do we make electric cars work at six hundred dollars a kilowatt-hour." It's "how fast can we walk the cost down toward the eighty-dollar floor." That's a ten-year program. That's a company. That's Tesla.

## Conclusion
We committed Tesla to a decade-long program of driving battery cost down toward the thermodynamic floor — new chemistries, new cell formats, the Gigafactory, in-house cell manufacturing, all of it. By 2020 we were under a hundred fifty a kilowatt-hour at pack level, heading for under eighty at cell level. The industry that told us in 2008 it was impossible is now operating at prices they said were physically impossible. Look — the thing most people miss is that "the price" and "the physics" are almost never the same number. Walk back to the physics.

## Abstract Form
When the industry quotes you a price as if it were a constant of nature, treat that number as the symptom, not the diagnosis. Decompose the product to its raw materials at commodity spot price. That sum is the thermodynamic floor. The gap between the quoted price and the floor is not fundamental — it is the accumulated inefficiency of an industry that nobody has audited from the atoms up. In almost every industry I have ever looked at, this gap is a factor of three to ten. The key signals that the industry price is inflated: (a) everyone in the industry quotes the same number and treats it as given, (b) nobody can tell you why it is that number rather than half that number, (c) the number has not moved materially in a decade while the underlying materials have changed. Diagnostic question: what is the bill-of-materials cost at commodity spot price, today? If the answer is wildly below the quoted price, there is a company in the gap. Your job is to walk the price down toward the floor. Most industries will tell you this is impossible. Most industries are wrong about this, consistently, because most industries are in a local minimum held together by the shared assumption that the local minimum is the floor.
