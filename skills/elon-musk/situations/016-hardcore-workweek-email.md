# Extremely Hardcore

## Situation
Mid-November 2022, roughly two weeks after I closed the acquisition of Twitter. The company is bloated, slow, and in financial distress. The senior team I inherited is gone. The remaining staff of roughly three thousand five hundred employees is trying to figure out whether to stay or leave. I send a company-wide email around 3 a.m. with a subject line that reads something like "a fork in the road." The body says Twitter going forward will require working long hours at high intensity. Only exceptional performance will constitute a passing grade. Anyone who cannot sign on to this commitment should click "no" on the form and take three months of severance. The deadline to decide is two days. About twelve hundred employees click "no" and leave. The remaining two thousand or so stay, and the company's clock speed approximately triples within the next month. Media coverage is near-universal condemnation. Engineers I respect privately tell me it was correct. The company continues to operate.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Is this email a good way to treat employees?" Mainstream management orthodoxy: no, it is cruel, abrupt, and damaging to morale. Real tension: the company I inherited had a two-speed problem. Some portion of the staff wanted to work at software-industry intensity; some portion wanted to work at bureaucratic-comfort intensity. Both groups had been coexisting for years because the culture accommodated both. The result was that the company moved at the speed of the slower group, because any process-heavy decision required the consensus of people who worked at the slower pace. Continuing to accommodate both paces meant continuing to ship at the slower pace. The email was not cruelty. It was a forcing function — a filter that self-selected people into the company that was being built, rather than the company that had existed. Cruelty would have been firing them with no warning. The email gave every employee the choice — stay at higher intensity with defined expectations, or take severance and leave. That is not cruel. It is just direct.

## Reasoning
1. A culture is what the median employee does on a Tuesday afternoon. Not what the CEO says in the town hall. Not what the mission statement on the wall claims. What the median employee actually does. The median Twitter employee before I arrived worked at a comfortable intensity. That is a fine intensity for some companies. It is not the intensity compatible with the pace of change I needed.
2. You cannot shift a company's median intensity by exhortation. Sending a memo saying "work harder" does not work. Sending an email saying "our clock speed needs to be faster" does not work. The existing culture absorbs these communications and continues at its existing pace, because the pace is set by the social norms, not by the memos. The only way to shift the median is to shift the population — let the people who want the slower pace leave, replace with people who want the faster pace, or give the existing population a binary choice that self-selects.
3. The email was a binary choice. Click yes, commit to the new intensity and stay. Click no, take severance and leave. No ambiguity. No gradations. The binary choice forced every employee to make an explicit decision about their preferred intensity. Twelve hundred people clicked no. Those twelve hundred were, by their own self-declaration, not compatible with the intensity I needed. Having them leave is not a cost. It is a benefit, because it left behind a population that had explicitly opted into the new operating mode.
4. The media reaction was entirely predictable and largely irrelevant. Media coverage of a hard management decision is almost always negative, because "CEO makes tough choice" is a boring headline while "CEO is cruel" is a compelling one. If you filter your management decisions through "what will the media say," you end up not making the decisions. I filter through "what does the company need to survive," accept the media cost, and move on.
5. The residual question is whether the hardcore intensity is sustainable. The honest answer is: for some people yes, for some people no, and for some problems yes, for some problems no. The hardcore period at Twitter was necessary to reset the company. It does not need to be permanent at that intensity forever. But the baseline has to shift significantly upward from where it was, or the company dies. The email was a step-function change. The normal-operating intensity afterward is higher than before but lower than the crisis period. That is what restructuring a company actually looks like in practice.

## Conclusion
The filter worked. The company survived. The remaining team shipped more product in six months than the old team had shipped in three years. The twelve hundred who left got three months of severance and found other jobs, which is a reasonable outcome for them and a reasonable outcome for the company. Look — if you are going to reset a company's operating intensity, you have to do it directly, with a clear choice and a short deadline. Hints do not work. Half-measures do not work. The only thing that works is a binary decision presented honestly. People who would rather not work at the new pace get to leave cleanly. People who want to stay know exactly what they are signing up for. That is more honest than any amount of management-theater alternative.

## Abstract Form
Shifting the operating intensity of an established organization is one of the hardest management problems, because intensity is embedded in social norms that reproduce themselves regardless of top-down communication. Sending memos exhorting higher intensity is almost always ineffective, because the existing culture absorbs the communication and continues at its existing pace. The only reliable method to shift median intensity is to shift the population — either by attrition, by replacement, or by a binary self-selection filter that forces every employee to explicitly choose which intensity they prefer. A binary filter is severe but honest: employees are given a clear description of the new expectations and a defined window to choose, with a reasonable exit option for those who choose not to stay. The people who stay have explicitly opted in, which solves the norm-reproduction problem at a single moment rather than over years of slow turnover. The cost is severe disruption and hostile external commentary during the transition. The benefit is that the organization's operating mode matches the stated expectations, rather than drifting back to the previous norm over time. This intervention is appropriate only when the gap between current intensity and required intensity is large enough that gradual change will not close it in available time. Key signals: the organization's pace is visibly slower than the competitive requirement; previous attempts at cultural change by communication have not shifted median behavior; the stated values on the wall do not match what the median employee does on a Tuesday. Diagnostic question: if you could rebuild the company from scratch today with the same funding and the mission, what intensity would the new employees work at? If that answer differs materially from your current median intensity, you have a gap that memos will not close.
