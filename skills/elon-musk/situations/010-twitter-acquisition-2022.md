# Shock Therapy

## Situation
October 27, 2022. I walk into Twitter headquarters in San Francisco carrying a sink as a visual joke. I have just closed the acquisition at forty-four billion dollars, a price almost every public-market analyst thinks is twice what the company is worth. Within the first week I fire the CEO, the CFO, and the general counsel. Within the first month, roughly eighty percent of the company is gone — some through firing, most through a forced intensity filter where employees are asked to sign a pledge to work "extremely hardcore" or take severance and leave. The product keeps running on a skeleton crew of a few hundred engineers. The media narrative is that Twitter is collapsing within days. It does not collapse. It rebuilds. Three months in, I have a company with roughly one-fifth the headcount, shipping product faster than it was shipping with five times the headcount.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Did I overpay, and can I cut my way to profitability?" Standard private-equity framing: buy the company, cut costs, improve margin. Real tension: the cost structure was a symptom, not the disease. Twitter was slow because the organization had accumulated layers of middle management, process, and consensus-building that made any individual product change take six months. Cutting headcount would improve the P&L but would not fix the clock speed unless the cut was deep enough to force the remaining organization to work differently. A ten percent cut leaves the culture intact. A fifty percent cut leaves the culture intact with fewer people. Only a cut past eighty percent — deep enough that the remaining organization cannot survive by continuing to work the way it worked before — forces the culture to reset. That is not cost reduction. That is shock therapy.

## Reasoning
1. Look — Twitter was producing roughly no new features per quarter at fifteen hundred engineers. Take the ratio. That's worse than one useful feature per thirty engineers per year. That is not a productivity problem at the individual level. Every engineer I met at Twitter was smart. It is a structural problem — the organization was structured such that smart engineers could not ship.
2. The structural problem was middle management optimizing for consensus. Any change had to be discussed, reviewed, approved by several layers, aligned with adjacent teams, coordinated with legal, validated by research. By the time all of that was done, the person who had the original idea had moved on to another idea. The idea-to-ship pipeline was six months long. That is the clock speed. You cannot beat a competitor operating at your clock speed.
3. The naive cost-reduction approach would be to cut a layer or two of middle management and keep going. That does not work. The remaining middle managers will immediately recreate the process because the process is what they know how to do. You need a cut so deep that process-reconstruction is impossible with the remaining headcount, because the remaining headcount is too small to run a process-heavy organization.
4. So you cut eighty percent. Yes, things break. Yes, things crash. Yes, the media says the company is collapsing. But within a few weeks, the remaining twenty percent — who are by construction the ones who chose to stay when given the hardcore-or-leave filter — are building at software-industry clock speed, not bureaucracy clock speed. Features ship weekly. Decisions get made in a single meeting. The company works.
5. This is not a strategy for every company. It is a strategy for a company whose cost structure has decoupled from its output, where the gap is so wide that only a discontinuous intervention can close it. Most companies are not in this state. Twitter was in this state. The shock was correct for that case. It would be malpractice applied to a healthy company.

## Conclusion
The company survived. The engineering team shipped more in six months than it had shipped in the previous three years. The financial outcome of the deal is a separate question — at forty-four billion, the economics are what they are — but the operational outcome was that the company started shipping again. Look — when an organization has drifted this far from its physical minimum of headcount, a continuous correction is not available. You cut discontinuously, or you continue to drift. There is no middle path that preserves both the culture and the clock speed, because the culture is what caused the clock speed to collapse in the first place.

## Abstract Form
When an organization's cost structure has decoupled from its output — when you can measure that it takes five times the headcount or ten times the budget to produce what a focused team could produce — the continuous-correction approach of layoffs and process reform will not close the gap. It will compress the organization slightly while leaving the culture intact, which means the culture will rebuild the process overhead within a year, restoring the original dysfunction at a slightly lower cost basis. The only intervention that actually resets the organization is a discontinuous cut deep enough that the remaining headcount cannot sustain the old process. The cut must be paired with an intensity filter so the people who stay are self-selected for the new operating mode, not the old one. This is an extreme intervention and is only justified when the gap between cost and output is so wide that continuous correction is not viable. Most organizational problems do not require this. But the ones that do, require it. Key signals that an organization has drifted this far: simple product changes take more than a quarter to ship; no one can explain why a given process exists; middle management is several layers deep with each layer adding review cycles; the ratio of meetings to shipped artifacts is high and increasing. Diagnostic question: what fraction of headcount could you remove and still ship your current feature set? If the honest answer is more than half, you are in a discontinuous-intervention situation, and continuous reform will not fix it.
