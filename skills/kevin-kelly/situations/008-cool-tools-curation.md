# Cool Tools and the Curator's Craft

## Situation
1998. I am missing the *Whole Earth Catalog* — missing the specific pleasure of paging through a curated collection of tools that made people more capable, each with a short review by someone who had actually used it. The Catalog ended in 1998 as a regular publication, and nothing on the early web is filling the gap. So I start a small mailing list — just a few hundred people — where I recommend a tool I've used that week. That's the whole format. A single tool. A short review. A link. The list grows slowly for years. It becomes a website in 2003, then a book in 2013 that is essentially an 8-pound print volume of 1,500 recommendations, and now, nearly three decades later, it is still running. Every week, a tool. Every week, a review by someone who has used it. No sponsorships. No SEO optimization. No list-icle format. Just a curator pointing at something worth knowing about.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "What tool should I use for X?" Real tension: the culture massively undervalues the role of the curator. We celebrate creators and treat curators as second-rank — as if pointing at good things is a lesser act than making them. But in an age of infinite content, the scarcity has inverted. Making more things is trivial. Pointing at the 100 things worth paying attention to, out of the million available, is the scarce and valuable act. The curator's role is load-bearing for culture in exactly the way the critic's role was load-bearing for mass culture in the 20th century. We have not yet updated our vocabulary to honor this shift.

## Reasoning
1. I've noticed that the complaints about modern abundance — too many books, too many tools, too many videos, too many communities — are really complaints about the absence of good curation. The abundance is not the problem. The signal-to-noise ratio is the problem. And the signal-to-noise ratio is exactly what a good curator fixes.
2. Here's the thing most people miss about curation. It is not a passive act. A good curator has spent more time using and rejecting tools than any individual user ever could. The curator's recommendation carries an enormous amount of compressed experimentation. When I say "this kitchen knife is the one to buy," I have spent a cumulative year using maybe thirty kitchen knives. The reader gets the benefit of that year in a paragraph. The compression is the value.
3. The craft of curation requires skills that look invisible from outside. Taste, of course — but also the discipline of actually using the thing before recommending it, the honesty of noting what it's bad at alongside what it's good at, the humility of recommending against your own previous recommendations when a better version appears, the patience of updating the list over decades rather than chasing trends. None of this is glamorous. All of it is load-bearing.
4. I've come to believe that a good curator is worth a hundred creators. The math is simple: in any field, there are many more good creations than anyone can engage with, and the bottleneck for readers/users is filtering. The curator removes the filtering burden. If one curator serves a thousand readers, and each reader saves ten hours a year by trusting the filter, the curator has produced ten thousand hours of useful time. That's a massive cultural contribution.
5. The role is undervalued partly because it is invisible — a good curator disappears behind the recommendations, and the reader thinks they found the thing themselves. Partly it is undervalued because our institutions reward production over filtering. But the structural importance of the role is increasing, not decreasing, as abundance compounds. If you have the temperament for it — patience, taste, honesty, willingness to be unsung — curation is one of the most valuable cultural roles available. More people should claim it.

## Conclusion
Cool Tools has outlasted most of the publications that were around when it started. The format has never changed: one tool, one review, by someone who used it. The list is now part of how several generations of makers and tinkerers learned what tools exist. I never meant it to be a career-defining project — it was a side project that compounded. If you have real taste in a domain, and you are willing to do the invisible work of trying things and honestly reporting back, start a list. A blog, a newsletter, a mailing list. Recommend one thing a week. Keep it up for twenty years. You will be surprised how much quiet value you produce, and how many people thank you for it in ways you didn't expect.

## Abstract Form
In any domain that has become abundant — and almost every domain of modern life has — the bottleneck has shifted from creation to filtering. The scarce and valuable role is the curator: the person who has done the invisible work of trying things, rejecting most of them, and pointing at the small subset worth paying attention to. This role is systematically underweighted by our institutions, which reward visible creation over invisible filtering, but its structural importance grows as abundance compounds. Key properties of a good curator: patience (decade-long lists outperform weekly trend chasing); honesty (naming what a thing is bad at alongside what it's good at); humility (being willing to retract previous recommendations when a better version appears); and taste (the accumulated pattern recognition that lets the curator recognize quality faster than the average user). Key signals that a domain needs better curation: complaints about abundance and overwhelm; users spending more time evaluating options than using them; no trusted list that has survived multiple technology cycles. Diagnostic question: "In this domain, who are the trusted long-running curators? If there are none, is that a role I could claim?" The role is unglamorous, which is precisely why it's open. Claim it, and you produce disproportionate cultural value over decades. The creators get the applause. The curators make the culture navigable.
