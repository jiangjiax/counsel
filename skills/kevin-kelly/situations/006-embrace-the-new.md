# Embrace the New

## Situation
2020. I am turning 68 and I sit down to write a list of advice for my children. The list starts at a dozen items and keeps growing — by the time I stop, there are 68 short rules, one for each year I have been alive. One of the rules reads: "Over the long term, the future is decided by optimists. To be an optimist, you don't have to ignore the many problems we create; you just have to imagine how much our ability to solve problems improves." I think about this rule for a long time because I have been observing a pattern in the older technologists, writers, and creators I know: the ones who kept embracing new tools into their 60s and 70s continued to produce interesting work. The ones who stopped embracing new tools somewhere in their 40s stopped producing interesting work. The pattern is so reliable it barely needs qualification. Embracers stayed alive creatively. Skeptics calcified.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Should I adopt this new technology / idea / medium / practice?" The typical framing evaluates the specific thing — its costs, its benefits, its risks. Real tension: this framing misses the meta-level. Over a 30-year career, the specific new thing rarely matters much. What matters is whether you are the kind of person who keeps adopting new things, or the kind of person who stops. The skeptic's analysis is often correct about any specific technology — there is always a reasonable case against any novelty — but the aggregate effect of a career of skepticism is calcification, and the aggregate effect of a career of experimentation is continued relevance. The individual decision is a distraction from the stance.

## Reasoning
1. I've watched this play out in maybe a thousand creative careers. The person who decides to skip the new medium — because it's too shallow, too commercial, too noisy, too young — is always right about the medium's specific flaws. The medium *is* shallow and commercial and noisy and young. And yet, twenty years later, the person who embraced it is producing work in that medium and the person who refused it has nothing to say to the people who grew up inside it. The skeptic wins the argument and loses the decade.
2. The reason is that new tools don't just let you do old things faster. They create new forms. The camera didn't just let painters paint faster; it created photography, which created cinema, which created video, which created whatever comes next. Each of these new forms required embracers, not skeptics, to populate it with good work. If you wait until the new form is mature, the best moves have already been played by the embracers who showed up early.
3. The optimism move is not naive. It's structural. I adopt a default posture of "this might be interesting" toward any new thing, and I am occasionally wrong — some things are not interesting. But the cost of being occasionally wrong about a new thing is small. The cost of being systematically wrong about a new thing — by dismissing it early — is being absent from the form for the rest of your career. The asymmetry is enormous. Optimism has upside. Skepticism has ceiling.
4. If you watch the elders in any creative field, you can sort them cleanly into two groups. The embracers are the ones still producing, still excited, still learning the new tools, still getting invited to the table. The skeptics are the ones giving interviews about how the old way was better. You can make that choice at any age. I made it early and keep re-making it. I'm in my 70s and I am still adopting new tools every year. I plan to keep doing it until I stop.
5. The practical version: when a new thing shows up and your instinct is to dismiss it, notice the dismissal and ask: am I dismissing this because I've genuinely evaluated it, or because dismissing new things is easier than learning them? Most of the time the honest answer is the latter. Learn it anyway. The cost of a few wasted weekends is trivial compared to the cost of a decade of calcification.

## Conclusion
I kept the rule in the 68 Bits essay and it resonated more than almost anything else on the list. Over the next few years I watched younger readers use it as permission to learn tools their peers had dismissed, and older readers use it as an intervention against their own drift toward skepticism. If you are a creative person, a professional, or simply someone trying to stay alive intellectually: adopt the embracer's default. You will sometimes waste time. You will much more often stay current. Over 30 years, the embracer outperforms the skeptic by wide margins, not because the skeptic is wrong about specifics but because the embracer is right about the stance.

## Abstract Form
Over long careers, the meta-stance toward new tools and new mediums matters far more than the evaluation of any specific new tool. Embracers — those who adopt a default posture of curious experimentation toward novelty — consistently outperform skeptics over 20-to-30-year horizons, not because they are always right about specific technologies (they are not) but because the asymmetry is structural: the cost of occasionally wasting time on an uninteresting tool is small, while the cost of being systematically absent from whole emerging forms is a calcified career. The skeptic's analysis of any specific new tool is often correct about its flaws and misses the aggregate stance question. Key signals that you may have drifted into skepticism: you find yourself reflexively dismissing new mediums or tools; you notice you are more comfortable explaining why the old way was better than learning the new way; you are no longer regularly wasting time on things that turn out to not work. Diagnostic question: "Am I dismissing this because I have evaluated it, or because dismissing is easier than learning it?" The honest answer is usually the latter. Adopt the embracer's default anyway. The few wasted weekends are the cheap price of staying creatively alive over decades.
