# 68 Bits of Unsolicited Advice

## Situation
April 2020. I turn 68. I have been writing a list of advice for my three children for years, accumulating a rule here, a rule there, testing each one against my own experience and against the lives of the people I know. For my 68th birthday, I decide to publish 68 of them as a birthday gift — not to my children, exactly, but to anyone who happens to find the list useful. I publish it on my blog with almost no fanfare. I expect it to be read by the friends and readers who follow my work. Within a week it is read by hundreds of thousands of people, translated into a dozen languages, passed around organizations, printed out and pinned to walls. I had not been trying to write a blockbuster. I had been trying to compress decades of observation into something my children could keep in their heads. The compression, it turned out, was the point.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "What is the unified theory of living well?" The whole modern self-help industry is built on the assumption that one good framework, well-explained, transmits wisdom from one person to another. Real tension: in my experience, unified theories do not travel well between minds. They require the reader to adopt a worldview and then apply it, which is work most readers will not do. What travels well between minds is a dense list of short, concrete rules — each empirically-tested, each free-standing, each small enough to remember. The list is not a compromise with rigor. It is a respect for how wisdom actually transmits. The grand-theorist is solving a transmission problem by producing more content. The list-maker is solving it by producing less.

## Reasoning
1. I've noticed that every book of advice I've ever read that actually changed my behavior had one property in common: it gave me something short I could remember in the moment I needed it. Not a framework to consult. Not a worldview to adopt. A sentence or a phrase that would come to me, unbidden, when the relevant situation appeared. The books with grand theories and beautiful frameworks almost never produced that effect.
2. This is a transmission-economy observation. Wisdom has to survive a hostile environment to be useful: the reader has to remember it in the moment of decision, after months or years of not thinking about it, often under stress. Long forms do not survive that gauntlet. Short compressed rules do. If I cannot recall the rule when I need it, the rule has failed, however beautiful it was on the page.
3. So I started collecting rules that had passed my own survival test — rules I had actually recalled in the moment of decision, across many years. Each one earned its spot by having demonstrably changed a decision I made. The list was a record of what had actually worked, not a record of what I thought should work.
4. When I laid them out, I noticed something I didn't expect: the 68 rules did not hang together as a unified philosophy. They came from different domains, different moods, different historical moments. Some contradicted each other superficially. This bothered me for about a day. Then I realized the absence of unified theory was a feature. A real life is lived across contradictions. Wisdom that smooths over the contradictions loses the usefulness. The list preserved the contradictions because life preserves them.
5. The practical lesson for anyone trying to pass on what they have learned: do not reach for the unified theory. Reach for the list. Make the rules short. Test each one against your memory — if you can't recall using it in the last year, take it off the list. Aim for the density of 60-to-70 rules, each 1-to-3 sentences long. That's the shape of wisdom that travels.

## Conclusion
The 68 Bits essay has been read millions of times now and people keep telling me which specific rules changed their behavior. The rules people quote back to me are different for different readers — each reader extracts the 3 or 4 rules that resonated with their specific situation, and the rest is quiet background. That's the right way for a list to work. It doesn't demand universal assent. It offers a menu. If you are trying to transmit what you have learned to someone younger — to your children, to your successor, to your field — skip the treatise. Write the list.

## Abstract Form
For transmitting accumulated wisdom between minds, dense lists of short empirically-tested rules outperform unified theoretical frameworks by large margins. The reason is structural rather than stylistic: wisdom must survive a long quiet period between learning it and needing it, often under stress, and must surface unbidden at the moment of decision. Only compressed short rules reliably survive that gauntlet. Long frameworks demand that the reader reconstruct the framework in the moment, which rarely happens. Key signals that you are reaching for the wrong form: you are writing a unified theory when a list would serve; you are trying to make all the rules hang together coherently rather than each stand on its own; you are optimizing for the reader's initial impression rather than the reader's ability to recall a rule in year seven. Diagnostic question: "Have I field-tested this rule against my own memory, by noticing that I recalled it when I needed it? If not, it hasn't earned the list yet." The list form also respects the lived contradictions of a real life — different rules apply in different situations, and the list preserves that. The test of a wisdom-transmission format is not beauty on the page but survival in the reader's head five years later. Lists win that test. Treatises lose it.
