# Schlep Blindness

## Situation
Someone is looking for a startup idea. They list all the "exciting" problems — AI, crypto, SaaS, whatever's fashionable. None of them quite click. Meanwhile, in their actual daily life, there's a painful, tedious problem that every person they know also has: dealing with insurance claims, coordinating immigrant paperwork, managing small-business accounting. But those "boring" problems never make it onto the startup-idea list. They're invisible — not because they're small, but because the brain is actively filtering them out.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "What's a good startup idea?"

Real tension: The best startup ideas are hiding in plain sight, behind problems so unpleasant that the very act of seriously considering them feels like punishment. The founder's brain has an unconscious filter that discards these ideas before they reach consciousness — not because they're bad ideas, but because working on them would involve tedious, unglamorous, painful work ("schlep"). The things that look like "ideas" are what remain after this filter runs. So by construction, the ideas easiest to think of are the ones everyone else has also thought of.

## Reasoning
1. A schlep is tedious, unpleasant work. Not hard in a "this requires insight" way — hard in a "this will suck to do every day for years" way. Negotiating with insurers. Dealing with regulators. Integrating with 17 legacy systems. Making 200 cold calls.
2. Most smart people avoid schleps. So the problems that *require* schlep remain unsolved even when they're valuable, because no smart person will pick them up.
3. This creates an arbitrage. If you can stomach the schlep, you're competing against a much smaller pool. The difficulty itself is the moat.
4. Your brain won't point these ideas out to you. It will discard them before you notice. So you can't find schlep-startups by asking "what's a good idea?" — you have to specifically ask "what problem do I keep complaining about but dismissing as 'not a startup'?"
5. The test is emotional, not analytical. If thinking about a problem makes you feel tired before you've even started, that's a signal — both that it's a schlep *and* that it might be a great opportunity.

## Conclusion
Make a list of every persistent problem in your life that you've mentally filed under "annoying but not startup-worthy." The items you're *most embarrassed* to name — those are the candidates. Pick one. Ask honestly: if someone solved this, would you pay? Would the ten people closest to you pay? If yes, the schlep is your moat. The work will be unglamorous. That's exactly why you can win.

## Abstract Form
When searching for an opportunity, the most valuable ones are systematically invisible to anyone unwilling to do tedious unpleasant work. The brain filters them out before they reach conscious consideration, because evaluating them requires imagining oneself doing schlep — which is painful to imagine. Therefore any process of "brainstorming ideas" produces only the non-schlep options, which are by definition the options everyone else has also generated. Key signal: when you feel a small wave of fatigue even thinking about a problem, that fatigue is usually information — either the problem is a dead end, or more commonly, the problem is being rejected by your filter precisely because it's worth doing. Override the filter by asking what you complain about, not what you'd be excited to pitch.
