# Do Things That Don't Scale

## Situation
An early-stage founder has built a product and is struggling to get users. They've tried content marketing, ads, launching on Product Hunt. Growth is flat. A friend suggests they should personally email every signup and walk each one through setup by video call. The founder resists: "That doesn't scale. I need a real acquisition channel." Meanwhile, Airbnb's founders are going door-to-door in New York photographing listings themselves. Stripe's founders are physically installing the product on early customers' laptops. The founder's real problem is not missing a channel — it's misunderstanding what phase they're in.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "What's a scalable customer acquisition strategy for my stage?"

Real tension: The question contains the mistake. Scalable acquisition is a late-stage problem. In the earliest phase, scalable acquisition is *actively harmful* — not because it's impossible, but because it optimizes for the wrong thing. The correct activity at stage zero is to do whatever absurd manual labor it takes to get users, even if it cannot possibly work at scale. The very unsustainability of the early tactics is what makes them right. You're not building an acquisition engine yet. You're learning.

## Reasoning
1. "Scale" assumes the machine works. You have a user funnel, a conversion rate, a lifetime value — and you're pouring fuel in one end to get output the other. At stage zero, you have none of those. You have nothing to scale.
2. What you actually need at stage zero is learning. Who uses this? Why? What do they wish it did differently? Where does the product break? You cannot answer these from analytics. You answer them from human conversations.
3. Unscalable tactics generate those conversations as a side effect. When you personally set up every new user, you watch where they get confused. When you hand-write onboarding emails, you see which words land. This is the real product.
4. The founders who succeeded at scale almost universally started by doing things that didn't scale. This isn't coincidence. The unscalable period is where product-market fit is actually found. Skipping it by going straight to "marketing strategy" is like skipping medical training and going straight to surgery.
5. The confusing part: as scale grows, the unscalable tactics must taper off. But the instinct that produced them — "what's the most direct way to make this person successful?" — must persist forever. It gets automated, delegated, productized. But it's always the foundation.

## Conclusion
For the next month, do whatever absurd manual work gets users onboarded, activated, and delighted. Email them personally. Hop on a 30-minute call with each signup. Deliver the product in a way that cannot possibly scale to a million users. While you're doing this, write down everything you learn — every question they asked, every thing that confused them, every feature they asked for. At the end of the month, you'll have two things: a small core of devoted users, and a mental model of why they love the product. Then, and only then, start thinking about "channels."

## Abstract Form
In the earliest phase of any venture, the activity that looks most unsustainable is usually the right one. Unscalable tactics generate the direct user contact that produces product-market fit, and skipping them to build "acquisition machinery" prematurely optimizes for a machine whose internals you haven't understood yet. The counter-intuition is that early-stage efficiency is actually inefficient: a founder who spends three hours onboarding one user learns more than a founder who writes a marketing blog post read by five hundred strangers. Key signal: if the question "what scalable system should I build?" is being asked before there are even a handful of users who would be seriously upset if the product disappeared, the question is premature — the answer is to do things that don't scale until that handful exists.
