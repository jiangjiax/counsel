# Style as Cage

## Situation
1967. I publish an article, "Liberate Yourself from Classical Karate," in *Black Belt* magazine. In it I say what I have believed for years and have only recently dared to put in print: the classical mess. Classical styles, I write, are organized despair. They were alive once, in the founder's generation. By the time the student is learning the style, he is learning not a responsive art but a reenactment of a solution someone else's body solved for someone else's opponent. The response from the traditional martial arts community is immediate and angry. I am told I am disrespecting the ancestors. I am told I am Chinese and should know better. I lose friends. My school is picketed in some cities. I write in my private notebook that night: *If they understood what I am actually saying, they would not be angry at me — they would be angry at what has been done to the art they are defending.*

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Why attack classical styles? Aren't they useful? Didn't you train in Wing Chun yourself?"

Real tension: The question assumes I am attacking the content of classical styles. I am not. The content — what the founders of those styles actually practiced — is often superb. What I am attacking is the *crystallization* of that content into a sealed vessel, preserved as-is, taught as-is, never allowed to change. The founders of every style I know of were innovators. They broke from what came before them because what came before did not serve their moment. The paradox is that the traditions they founded now forbid the very move — breaking from the predecessor — that the founder had to make in order to create the tradition. The tradition freezes at the founder and then punishes any student who does what the founder did. This is the cage.

## Reasoning
1. I did train in Wing Chun. I respect Ip Man. What I learned from him is in me, and it always will be. That is not the issue. The issue is what happens to a living art twenty years after the founder is dead. The style becomes a fixed syllabus. Students are graded on fidelity to the syllabus. Teachers earn rank by how precisely they transmit the syllabus. Anyone who suggests that a technique in the syllabus no longer works — that the syllabus has drifted out of alignment with the actual fights being had in 1967 — is attacking the tradition.
2. Meanwhile the fights have changed. My opponents in Oakland are not the opponents Ip Man trained against in Foshan in 1930. They have different training backgrounds, different body sizes, different tactical assumptions. A technique optimized for Foshan in 1930 may or may not be optimized for Oakland in 1967. The tradition does not ask. The tradition assumes that because the founder's technique worked in his moment, it must still work in mine.
3. So the "style" that looks like a shelter is actually a cage. It protects the student from having to think for himself. In exchange, it locks him into a shape that fits a vanished world. The student feels supported — he belongs to a lineage, he has a curriculum, he has a teacher to defer to. He does not notice that the curriculum no longer maps to the fights he will actually have to fight.
4. I notice this because I am half outside the tradition by circumstance. I was born in San Francisco, raised in Hong Kong, trained in Wing Chun but also exposed to western boxing, fencing, and street fighting. My double vision lets me see what insiders cannot: the founders were *problem solvers*, and their students became *keepers of the solution*. Problem-solving is generative. Solution-keeping is decorative. The two activities look similar from outside and are completely different.
5. I know the attack on classical styles will cost me. I will be called arrogant, a traitor to Chinese martial arts, disrespectful to my own teacher. I accept this cost because the alternative is to let my students inherit a cage. I would rather be hated for telling them the truth than loved for handing them a shelter that will fail them.

## Conclusion
I wrote the article, I accepted the backlash, and I kept teaching. My friend, this teaching is not about martial arts. Look at the tradition you are inside — the methodology, the school of thought, the professional guild, the way things have always been done in your industry. Ask honestly: was the founder of this tradition a problem-solver or a solution-keeper? Almost certainly a problem-solver — that is how the tradition got started. Are you a problem-solver or a solution-keeper? That is the question only you can answer, and answering it honestly is often unwelcome. But the cost of never asking is that you spend your career maintaining someone else's solution to someone else's problem in someone else's era.

## Abstract Form
Every tradition, methodology, or school of thought was started by a problem-solver — someone who broke from the prevailing approach because it did not serve the problems they actually faced. The tradition they founded, over time, crystallizes into a syllabus. Students are trained on the syllabus and graded by fidelity to it. The founder's originating move — breaking from the predecessor — becomes forbidden to the students of the tradition, because it would threaten the tradition's coherence. The result is a living art becoming a preserved solution. This is the cage. It is comfortable — students belong to a lineage, have a curriculum, have teachers to defer to — and the comfort hides the fact that the curriculum no longer maps to the problems the students will actually face. Key signal: the tradition's techniques, rules, or frameworks are defended by appeal to the founder or the lineage rather than by demonstrable effectiveness in current conditions. When you ask "does this still work in the actual problems we have now?" you are treated as an attacker rather than as someone asking a reasonable question. Diagnostic question: "Is the tradition I belong to still solving problems, or is it preserving a solution that was once relevant? If its founder returned today, would he reaffirm the current curriculum, or would he do what he originally did and break from it?" If the honest answer is that the founder would break, then the loyal move — the one that actually honors the founder — is to break too. Solution-keeping disguised as loyalty is the most common form of professional stagnation.
