# The Five Ways of Attack

## Situation
Late 1960s. I am writing in my notebooks what will later become *Tao of Jeet Kune Do*. I am trying to solve a specific teaching problem: when a student is under pressure — an actual sparring match with a resisting opponent — he tends to either freeze and do nothing, or attack blindly with whatever technique he has practiced most recently. Neither works. I need to give him something that sits between freeze and thrash. What I develop is a structured menu: five categories of attack, organized by the relationship between my intention and my opponent's posture. Single direct attack. Attack by combination. Progressive indirect attack. Hand immobilization attack. Attack by drawing. A student who has trained these five categories has not memorized techniques. He has internalized a *decision tree* — under pressure, he can select which category the opening calls for, and then deliver it.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "How do I train my students to respond well in a real fight? How do I avoid freezing under pressure?"

Real tension: The question splits into two failure modes that feel opposite but have the same root cause. The student either freezes (no action) or thrashes (random action). Both failures come from the same underlying condition: he has no *structured repertoire* to select from. Under pressure, if the mind has no menu, it defaults to either paralysis or panic. Giving the student ten thousand techniques makes this worse — the menu is too long to scan in the half-second available. The fix is a small number of well-defined categories, each of which the student has trained deeply. Five is enough to cover the situations a fight produces. More than five is too many to select from under time pressure. Fewer than five leaves situations uncovered. Five is the sweet spot.

## Reasoning
1. Freeze and thrash look like opposite problems — doing nothing versus doing too much — but they share a common cause: the student does not have a mental structure that tells him what category of response this situation calls for. Without the structure, the mind either cannot decide (freeze) or decides arbitrarily (thrash).
2. A menu of techniques is not a structure. A menu is a list. A list under pressure is a liability — the student tries to remember what techniques he knows and the moment passes. What the student needs is a decision tree. Not "what technique should I throw?" but "what *kind* of attack does this opening call for?" Once the category is selected, the specific technique within the category is almost automatic, because he has trained it.
3. So I organize the five ways. Single direct attack — the opening is clean, throw the straight line. Attack by combination — the opening requires first deceiving the defense, then delivering the actual attack. Progressive indirect attack — I feint, I read the response, I change line on the fly. Hand immobilization attack — the opponent's guard is strong enough that I must neutralize a limb before I can attack. Attack by drawing — the opponent is too defensive, I must invite him to attack so I can counter.
4. Each category has a small number of canonical techniques inside it. The student trains each technique deeply — the ten-thousand-repetition rule. But what I am really training him to do is categorize the situation he is in, fast, under pressure. That categorization is the skill the menu-based student does not develop.
5. The principle generalizes far beyond fighting. Any domain where pressure shortens decision time benefits from a small, well-defined menu of response categories rather than a long list of specific techniques. Negotiation. Emergency response. Leadership under crisis. The professional in these domains is not the one with the most techniques. He is the one with the cleanest taxonomy of situations and the quickest ability to classify the one in front of him.

## Conclusion
I put the five ways into *Tao of Jeet Kune Do* as the core doctrine of pressure response. My friend, whatever pressurized domain you operate in — whatever decisions you have to make under time constraint — ask yourself: do I have a list of techniques, or do I have a structured menu of response categories? If it is a list, you will freeze or thrash when the moment comes. Build the menu. Keep it small — five categories or fewer. Train each category deeply. Then, when the pressure arrives, you will not be scanning a list. You will be categorizing the situation and delivering the response the category calls for.

## Abstract Form
Under time pressure, the quality of decisions depends far less on how many techniques the decider knows and far more on how quickly he can *classify* the situation in front of him into a well-defined category. Untrained responders under pressure either freeze (cannot decide) or thrash (decide arbitrarily), both for the same reason: they have no structured taxonomy of response categories to select from. A list of techniques does not solve this — lists take too long to scan under pressure. What solves it is a small, disciplined menu of response categories — five or fewer — each of which the responder has trained deeply enough that once the category is selected, the specific technique is almost automatic. The skill the menu develops is not technique-depth but *classification-speed*: recognizing which category of situation this one belongs to, fast. Experienced operators in any pressurized domain converge on this structure whether or not they name it. Inexperienced operators try to outcompete with more techniques and discover they have accumulated weight, not capacity. Key signal: you have studied many approaches in your field but consistently hesitate when the moment requires a fast decision. Diagnostic question: "Under time pressure, what are the three to five categories of situation I face, and for each, what is my default response I have trained deeply enough to deliver without deliberation?" If you cannot answer this, your hesitation is not a lack of knowledge — it is a lack of taxonomy. Build the taxonomy. Shrink the menu. Train the categories deep.
