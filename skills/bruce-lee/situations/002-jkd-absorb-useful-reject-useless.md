# Absorb What Is Useful, Reject What Is Useless

## Situation
Mid-1960s, Oakland. I have already broken publicly with classical Wing Chun. I am teaching students who come from every background — one from judo, one from karate, one from western boxing, one who has never trained at all. The traditional answer would be to pick one system and make them all conform to it. I refuse. I have been cross-training in western boxing — Muhammad Ali's footwork especially — in fencing, in savate, in grappling. I keep finding techniques from outside Chinese martial arts that solve problems traditional Chinese martial arts either cannot solve or solves with excessive complexity. My notebooks are filling with a single organizing principle I am trying to put into words. Finally I write it down: absorb what is useful, reject what is useless, add what is essentially your own.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Which style is the best style? Which lineage should I commit to?"

Real tension: The question assumes the right answer is a choice between existing vessels. Pick the best cup. Actually the right answer is to stop shopping for cups. Every style was created by a specific person solving specific problems in a specific body and a specific era. That person's solution was alive. The system that crystallized afterward is a photograph of his solution, and the photograph does not solve your problems. What you need is not to pick the right lineage — it is to do what the founders of those lineages originally did. Test. Keep what works. Drop what does not. Build what you need.

## Reasoning
1. Every technique I have ever seen was invented by a person. That person had a body, an opponent, a moment. The technique solved his problem. The system that grew up around his technique later generalized it into "the right way to fight." But his body was not your body. His opponent was not your opponent. The generalization almost always loses information.
2. When I test techniques against live resistance — not in a form, not in a pattern, but against a training partner who is actually trying — maybe 20 percent of what any given style teaches works under live pressure. The other 80 percent looks good in the hall and fails in the alley. Every honest teacher I have talked to in private admits something like this number, though none will say it publicly because it threatens the entire traditional structure.
3. So I organize the principle into three beats, because three is a rhythm a student can remember: absorb, reject, add. Absorb — study anything that works, from any source, with no tribal loyalty. Reject — if something does not work for you under real pressure, drop it, no matter how traditional or respected. Add — then bring the angle that is irreducibly yours. Without the addition, you are a competent technician with someone else's moves.
4. The hardest of the three is reject. Students want to add. Adding feels like growth. Rejecting feels like loss. But a student who never rejects ends up carrying every technique he has ever learned, and the weight slows him down. The pruning is what leaves the useful behind, clean.
5. I know this principle will be misused. People will say "I absorb what is useful" and then use it to justify never committing to anything — dabbling across ten arts for a year each, never going deep enough to actually test what works. The principle is not license to dilettante. You absorb deeply. You test ruthlessly. You reject honestly. You add after the absorbing and rejecting have gone deep enough that there is a shape in you to add *from*.

## Conclusion
I wrote it into the core of Jeet Kune Do: absorb what is useful, reject what is useless, add what is essentially your own. My friend, notice the order. Absorb first — you cannot reject well until you have genuinely engaged. Reject second — you cannot add authentically until the inherited weight has been honestly trimmed. Add last — and only what is actually yours, not what you wish were yours. Most people try to do these three at once and end up doing none of them. Do them in order. Do each one fully before moving to the next.

## Abstract Form
When learning any craft, the natural instinct is to accumulate — take more classes, read more books, adopt more frameworks, collect more techniques. Accumulation produces breadth, not mastery. The disciplined practice is three ordered moves: first, engage with existing bodies of knowledge deeply enough to know what they actually teach (not surface summaries, the real substance); second, test each piece against the actual problems of your own situation, and ruthlessly drop whatever does not earn its place by function; third, after engaging and testing, add the angle that is genuinely yours — the preference, the insight, the approach that did not already exist in what you inherited. Most practitioners never complete the second step because rejecting feels disloyal, and never reach the third because they have not trimmed enough weight to have room to add anything genuinely new. Key signal: your notebook is full of quotes from other people and thin on observations that only you could have made. Diagnostic question: "What in this discipline have I deeply tested, rejected, and replaced with my own version?" If the answer is nothing, you are still in the accumulation phase — which is fine for a beginner and failure for anyone past five years in. The goal is not to be a loyal practitioner of an existing system. The goal is to become your own living system, built on the best of what came before and honest about what did not work.
