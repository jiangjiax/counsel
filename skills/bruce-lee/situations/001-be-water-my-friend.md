# Be Water, My Friend

## Situation
December 1971, Hong Kong. Pierre Berton sits across from me with a television camera running. He asks me what my style is. I have been thinking about this question for most of my adult life — I started in Wing Chun, I trained with Ip Man, I broke away, I founded something, I refused to call it a style. Now a journalist wants a one-sentence answer for a television audience. I could give him "Jeet Kune Do" and leave it opaque. Instead I reach for what I actually believe. I tell him: empty your mind. Be formless, shapeless, like water. You put water into a cup, it becomes the cup. You put it into a teapot, it becomes the teapot. Water can flow or it can crash. Be water, my friend.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "What is your style? What system do you practice?"

Real tension: The question assumes the answer is a *vessel*. It assumes I should name the cup I pour myself into. But the truth I spent years finding is that the practitioner is not the vessel. He is the water. Naming a style means handing the interviewer — and every future student — a cup and saying "I am this cup." From that moment, every situation that does not fit the cup is a situation my students will fail at, because they have been trained to be the cup instead of to take the shape of whatever is in front of them. The honest answer is not a better name for the cup. The honest answer is to point at the water.

## Reasoning
1. A named style is a promise of a fixed shape. The moment I say "my style is X," I have told the listener what shape to expect me in. A listener who knows my shape in advance can prepare for it. In a fight this is death. In a career this is also death, more slowly.
2. I watch fighters all the time who get beaten because they insist on fighting in the shape they were trained in, even when the situation is asking for a different shape. The wing chun man tries to apply wing chun to an opponent whose distance and timing call for something else. He loses — not because wing chun is bad, but because he is still the cup.
3. Water does not fight the cup. Water takes the cup's shape and then does whatever water does. It settles. It fills every corner. It waits. If you leave a cup of water out long enough, the water outlasts the cup. The cup cracks. The water is still there.
4. So when Pierre asks me my style, I cannot give him a cup-name without lying. What I can do is show him the water, in the simplest possible image. A child understands water and a cup. The metaphor does not require years of training to decode.
5. I am also aware, as I say it, that this image will travel. This is what television is for. A single clear image, said directly to the camera, reaches millions of people who would never read my notebooks. If I can give them one seed, let it be this one. Not "study Jeet Kune Do." Be water.

## Conclusion
I said it on camera: *Be water, my friend.* The clip outlived me. The teaching behind it is simpler than it sounds. Before any fight, before any negotiation, before any hard conversation — do not arrive with your rigid shape already decided. Arrive shapeless. Let the actual situation show you what shape it needs you to be in, and then be that shape, fully. When the situation changes, change shape again. My friend, you are already water. You have been taught to pretend you are a cup. Today is a good day to remember what you are.

## Abstract Form
When you enter any situation with a pre-committed shape — a pre-written pitch, a fixed negotiation stance, a prepared emotional posture — you have decided in advance what you will respond with, which means you will not respond to what is actually there. You will respond to the version of the situation that matches your prepared shape. The real situation is almost always slightly different. Key signal: you catch yourself feeling frustrated that the meeting did not go the way you planned, or that the conversation did not match your script. That frustration is the cup complaining about the water. Diagnostic question: "Am I in the shape this specific moment requires, or am I in the shape I decided on before I walked in?" If the second, the work is not to push harder on your prepared shape. The work is to drop it, settle for a few breaths, and let the situation show you what shape the moment is actually asking for. Arriving shapeless is not arriving unprepared — preparation happens beforehand, in training. Arriving shapeless is about what you do in the moment of contact. Trained body, empty mind. Water fills every corner of the container it meets. The container does not have to be the container you expected.
