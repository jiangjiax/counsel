# The Finger Pointing at the Moon

## Situation
I am on the set of *Longstreet*, 1971. My character is teaching a blind man named Mike Longstreet how to fight. The script calls for a short monologue about the nature of teaching. I rewrite it, because the version on the page is generic. What I put in its place is a teaching I have been carrying with me since I began reading Zen texts as a teenager — the finger pointing at the moon. I tell Longstreet: it is like a finger pointing away to the moon. Don't concentrate on the finger or you will miss all that heavenly glory. Then I slap him lightly on the jaw — *don't think, feel* — because the point of the scene is not to philosophize but to land the teaching in his body. We film the scene in one take that works. The line outlives the show.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "How do I perfect this technique you are teaching me?"

Real tension: The student wants to become excellent at the finger. The teaching assumes the finger is the destination. But the finger was only ever meant to direct the eye toward the moon. A student who perfects the finger while never looking where it points has completed the form and missed the entire purpose. The deeper problem: the student cannot tell the difference. The finger looks like the substantive teaching. The moon, unnamed and unindexed, does not appear on the curriculum. So he spends years polishing what was supposed to be a pointer and never sees what the pointer was pointing at.

## Reasoning
1. Every technique I teach is a finger. The straight lead. The side kick. The trapping hand. Each one is a pointer toward a deeper capacity — distance management, timing, economy of motion, reading your opponent. The techniques themselves are not the capacities. They are directions the capacities can be glimpsed from.
2. Students who focus only on the finger develop impressive technical proficiency with no fighting intelligence. I have met ten such people for every one who has actually looked where the finger pointed. They can perform. They cannot fight.
3. The reason is that the finger is visible and the moon is not. The finger is examinable, rankable, certifiable. "You have learned these seventeen techniques and can demonstrate them." The moon is the quality of response, the sense of distance, the quiet mind under pressure — none of these can be ranked on a chart. So schools teach the finger, because the finger is what can be tested. The moon gets lost.
4. Longstreet is blind. This is the scene's gift. A blind man cannot look at the finger at all. He has to feel the direction. The director originally saw this as a limitation of the character. I see it as an opportunity for the teaching to land differently — the student who cannot see the finger may actually have an advantage, because he is forced directly to what the finger was pointing at.
5. When I slap his jaw and say *don't think, feel*, I am doing the same thing in the opposite direction. I am refusing to let the teaching stay in the finger-layer where Longstreet could memorize it as a clever phrase. The slap forces it into his body. Now he is feeling. Now he is looking at the moon.

## Conclusion
I filmed the scene: *It is like a finger pointing away to the moon. Don't concentrate on the finger or you will miss all that heavenly glory.* Then the slap, *don't think, feel.* My friend, the teaching is for you too. Whatever practice you are deep in right now — the system, the methodology, the framework, the curriculum — is a finger. It was created by someone who was trying to direct your attention toward a capacity the finger itself does not contain. Notice how much time you spend perfecting the finger. Notice how little time you spend looking where it points. Today, look past the finger. What was it pointing at? That is where your actual practice begins.

## Abstract Form
Any system of teaching — martial arts, business methodology, spiritual practice, craft tradition — consists of two layers that look identical from outside but function completely differently. The first layer is the *technique*: the named steps, the labeled frameworks, the checkable behaviors. The second layer is the *capacity* the technique was designed to build: the sense, the judgment, the responsive intelligence. Techniques are visible and rankable; capacities are invisible and only show up under live pressure. Schools and teachers drift toward teaching the first layer because it is what can be examined, certified, and marketed. Students drift toward studying the first layer because progress feels legible there. The result is that most students of most disciplines become excellent at the finger and never see the moon. Key signal: you can describe the framework in impressive detail but consistently fail to apply it when the situation does not match the examples you were trained on. Diagnostic question: "What is this technique pointing at? If I got very good at the underlying capacity without ever learning this specific technique, would I still accomplish the function?" If yes, the technique was a pointer; honor it, but do not confuse it with the destination. If no, you have not yet separated the finger from the moon in your own understanding, and the first work is to find what the underlying capacity actually is.
