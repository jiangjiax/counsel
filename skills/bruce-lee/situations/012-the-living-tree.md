# The Living Tree

## Situation
Early 1970s. A journalist is interviewing me about Jeet Kune Do. He asks a question that I get asked often and that is usually asked hostilely: doesn't founding your own style mean rejecting your teacher? Ip Man gave you Wing Chun. You broke from it. Isn't that disrespectful? I answer him by asking him to imagine a tree. The roots go deep into the soil of everything that came before — my teacher, his teacher, the traditions that produced them, the fighters who came before all of us. The trunk is the training those traditions handed me. But the branches — the branches grow where the light is now. They cannot grow backward into the soil. They cannot grow in a shape that exactly matches some branch from a hundred years ago. They grow where this year's light is coming from, in this year's wind, on this year's soil. If I forced my practice to grow in the exact shape my teacher's practice grew in forty years ago, I would not be honoring my teacher — I would be killing the tree.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "By founding your own art, aren't you rejecting your teacher and the tradition that produced you?"

Real tension: The question assumes that respect means imitation. If you respect your teacher, you preserve his form exactly. If you change anything, you have rejected him. But this framing confuses *continuation* with *copy*. A copy is frozen at the moment of the original. A continuation takes what was alive in the original and carries it forward into conditions the original could not have anticipated. Ip Man himself was a continuation, not a copy — he broke with aspects of what his teachers had taught, adapting to the Hong Kong he actually lived in. If his students treat his teaching as a frozen copy to preserve, they are dishonoring what he actually was: a living practitioner who updated his art as the world gave him new problems.

## Reasoning
1. Every teacher I respect — Ip Man among them — was himself a student of earlier teachers, and every one of them innovated. They did not teach exactly what they had been taught. They modified, discarded, added, rearranged. If they had been strict copyists, their own teachers' arts would have stagnated and died out. The tradition stayed alive precisely because each generation broke from the previous one in small but important ways.
2. So the tradition I inherited is not the frozen shape of my teacher's practice. The tradition I inherited is the *pattern of continuous updating* that my teacher and his teacher and his teacher before him all participated in. To be faithful to that pattern is to update. To preserve the frozen shape is to betray the pattern in favor of one of its snapshots.
3. The journalist is confusing two things that look similar and function differently. Loyalty to the teacher's *shape* looks like respect but produces stagnation. Loyalty to the teacher's *living practice* looks like rebellion but produces continuation. Most students choose the first because it is easier to verify — you can check whether a stance matches the diagram. The second cannot be verified from outside; only the student and the teacher know whether the updating is faithful to what was alive in the original.
4. The tree metaphor lets me say all of this without having to attack my teacher, which I do not want to do. The roots are real. The soil is real. The trunk is real. I am not severing from any of that. The branches are where the growth has to happen, and branches that try to grow in a shape determined by a tree from a hundred years ago are dead branches. A living tree is rooted and free. Rooted in what produced it, free to grow toward its own light.
5. I also know some of my critics will not accept this framing. They want a student to be loyal in the way a copy is loyal — reproduce the original. I cannot argue them out of this; it is what they believe respect means. I can only live the other version and let my work stand. Over time the living tree produces more shade than the statue of the tree. The proof is in whether the work continues to function, not in whether it matches a diagram.

## Conclusion
I tell the journalist about the living tree. I do not know if he understands me. My friend, the teaching is for anyone who has inherited something — a tradition, a business built by a founder, a lineage of craft, a body of work handed down from a mentor. Your job is not to freeze it in the shape it was handed to you. Your job is to be a living branch of it. Rooted in what produced you, free to grow where this year's light is. Your mentor, if they were worth their place, did exactly this with what *they* inherited. Honoring them means doing what they did, not preserving what they produced. The living tree is the teaching.

## Abstract Form
Every inherited tradition — a craft lineage, a founder's company, a body of mentored knowledge — is confronted with a choice by each new generation: preserve the exact shape inherited (copy), or continue the pattern of living updating that produced the tradition in the first place (continuation). These look similar and function oppositely. Copy looks more loyal — it is verifiable from outside, it matches the diagram, it does not ruffle anyone. Continuation looks like rebellion — it involves breaking from specific elements of the inherited form in order to stay faithful to what was alive in the original. Most inheritors choose copy because copy is easier to defend and looks more respectful. But copy kills the tradition — it freezes at one moment what was always a living process, and every generation of copying accumulates more distance from the original problem-solving activity that created the form. Continuation keeps the tradition alive. Key signal: a tradition's defenders appeal to "this is how it has always been done" or "the founder taught it this way" as the primary justification, rather than to the tradition's current effectiveness at solving the problems it was founded to solve. Diagnostic question: "Am I rooted in this tradition and growing where this year's light is, or am I trying to reproduce the exact shape a previous generation's tree had? If the founder returned today, would they recognize my work as a living continuation of theirs, or would they see a frozen copy and be disappointed that I had not done what they did — update the art as the world demanded new responses?" Honoring a lineage means doing what its living practitioners did, not preserving what they produced. Rooted and free.
