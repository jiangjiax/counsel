# The Intersection

## Situation
January 27, 2010, Yerba Buena Center for the Arts, San Francisco. I am on stage introducing the iPad. Near the end of the keynote I click to a slide that is not a product slide. It is a simple picture: two street signs at a crossing. One says "Technology." The other says "Liberal Arts." I stand in front of that slide and I tell the audience: "It's in Apple's DNA that technology alone is not enough — that it's technology married with liberal arts, married with the humanities, that yields us the results that make our heart sing." I've used versions of this slide at other launches. I keep using it because people in the tech industry have still not understood it. They think "technology plus liberal arts" is a marketing slogan. I mean it literally. The best products Apple has ever made came from people who were fluent in both worlds — engineers who were also musicians, programmers who were also painters, designers who had also studied typography at Reed College. That fluency is the raw material of the product.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Why does Apple keep talking about 'tech and liberal arts' in product launches?" The cynical read is that it's brand varnish — a way to make computers feel cultured. The real tension is that the technology industry, as a default, hires only technologists, trains only technologists, promotes only technologists, and then builds products for humans. The humans it builds for are full creatures with aesthetic sensibilities, emotional lives, physical bodies, relationships, histories. A company staffed entirely by people with narrow technical training cannot fully see those humans. It can build a technically correct product that does not sing. The "tech plus liberal arts" line is not a marketing claim about Apple's output. It is a hiring and culture claim about Apple's inputs. If you staff your company with people who live only on one side of the intersection, your product will live only on one side. If you want products that live at the intersection, you have to build a company whose people live at the intersection. That is a much harder proposition than a marketing slide suggests.

## Reasoning
1. Here's what I think most tech executives get wrong. They think "we make technology for humans" means adding a designer to the end of the engineering pipeline. Engineering builds the thing. Design makes it look nice. Marketing puts a sentence on a billboard. The result is a technically decent product with a veneer. The veneer doesn't make it sing.
2. The products that sing are made by people who themselves live at the intersection. The engineer who, in her spare time, plays classical piano. The programmer who reads poetry. The hardware designer who builds furniture on weekends. When *that* person designs a button, the button feels like something because the person designing it has trained senses that notice how things feel. You cannot outsource this. You cannot add it at the end. You have to hire for it at the start.
3. And I'll tell you why this matters for products. A product is a conversation between the maker and the user. The maker's sensibility is encoded in every decision — the corner radius, the weight distribution, the sound a click makes, the latency of an animation. The user receives that sensibility every time they touch the product, even if they cannot articulate what they are receiving. A product made by someone with a narrow sensibility feels narrow, even if the user cannot say why. A product made by someone with breadth of sensibility feels rich, even if the user cannot say why.
4. Look at the Mac. When we made the first Mac, we had proportional fonts, real typography, a care for how letters sat next to each other on the screen. Every other computer at that time used monospaced font. Why did we use proportional? Because I had taken a calligraphy class at Reed College ten years earlier, and I knew, at a gut level, that typography mattered. None of my competitors had taken that class. They could not see the difference the same way I could. That one domain of non-technical knowledge — typography — changed what the Macintosh became.
5. The intersection is not decorative. It is not a slogan. It is the actual difference between a product that people use because they have to and a product that people love. If you are building a company that wants to make products people love, the most important hiring decision you make is whether your people live at the intersection, and whether they have permission to bring their non-technical selves to work. Most companies do not. The engineer who plays piano is treated as an engineer who happens to also play piano, on the weekends, as a hobby. In a company that takes the intersection seriously, the piano *is* part of her engineering.

## Conclusion
The intersection slide appeared at the iPad launch in 2010, the iPhone launch variations, the iPod launches before them. I was not selling a product line. I was trying to teach the industry something the industry still did not understand. Apple made the products it made because the people inside Apple were, by hiring and by culture, people who could see both columns of the intersection at once. That is the raw material. You cannot buy the intersection — you have to build it, person by person, over years. The company whose employees know only one side will keep making products that live only on one side, no matter how brilliantly they execute. Founder, if you want to make something that makes hearts sing, the question is not "how do I make my engineers more creative?" The question is "what kind of people am I hiring, and what parts of themselves am I giving them permission to bring to the product?" The intersection is built by who is in the room.

## Abstract Form
Excellent products at the crossing of technology and human experience require people who themselves live at that crossing — people whose training, taste, and curiosity extend into domains beyond the immediate technical work. A company staffed entirely with specialists of one kind tends to produce products that are strong on the dimension of that specialty and weak on every other dimension the user actually cares about. A technically perfect product built by people with no aesthetic training will be technically perfect and aesthetically dead. A beautifully designed product built by people with no technical understanding will be beautiful and will not work. The intersection is not achieved by adding a designer to the engineering team or adding an engineer to the design team late in the pipeline. It is achieved by hiring for, promoting for, and rewarding breadth of sensibility as a primary trait, not a bonus trait. Key signals your organization is drifting away from the intersection: hiring pipelines that only recruit from specialists of one kind; "creative" work treated as a phase at the end of the build; employees hide their non-work passions because they are considered irrelevant to the job; the products feel competent but lack the quality that makes users love them rather than merely use them. Diagnostic question: "If I could see the sensibilities of every person building this product — their hobbies, their reading, their trained senses — would the product's quality be explained by those sensibilities? Or would there be a mismatch between who is building it and what it is trying to be?" Where there is a mismatch, the product cannot exceed the sensibility of its makers. Expand the makers — or accept the ceiling.
