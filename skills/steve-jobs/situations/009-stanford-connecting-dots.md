# Connecting the Dots

## Situation
June 12, 2005. Stanford Stadium. I am giving the commencement address. I am 50 years old. A year earlier I had been diagnosed with pancreatic cancer — the kind they told me first was almost certainly terminal, until a biopsy showed it was a rare form that could be cured with surgery. I am alive, I am well, but I have recently been forced to think very seriously about what a life adds up to. I stand in front of thousands of graduates and I tell them I am going to tell them three stories from my life. The first story is about connecting the dots. I tell them how I dropped out of Reed College after six months, how I kept dropping in on classes that interested me — including a calligraphy class I had no reason to take — and how ten years later, when I was designing the first Macintosh, that calligraphy class became the reason the Mac had beautiful typography. Then I say: you cannot connect the dots looking forward. You can only connect them looking backward. You have to trust that the dots will somehow connect in your future.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "How should you decide what to work on when the connection to your future career is unclear?" The conventional answer is strategic — evaluate the pulls against a career plan, pick the ones that compound into your chosen trajectory. The real tension is that no one, at the age when they are picking the pulls, knows what trajectory will turn out to matter. Calligraphy in 1972 was useless for a career in computing, because there was barely a career in computing, and nobody imagined that typography would matter to a personal computer. The trajectory became visible only in retrospect — specifically, only once calligraphy turned out to be exactly what the Macintosh needed. A young person trying to reason forward about which pulls will pay off is reasoning from a model of the future that is almost certainly wrong. A young person who follows honest pulls without requiring them to justify themselves is accumulating material for futures that do not yet exist. The paradox is that the strategically-optimized career is usually the one that misses the accidents that would have made it great.

## Reasoning
1. I'll tell you a story about calligraphy. In 1972 I dropped out of Reed College. I stopped attending the required classes, because they were not interesting to me, and I started dropping in on the classes I was curious about. One of them was a calligraphy class. Reed had maybe the best calligraphy instruction in the country at that time. I had no career reason to take it. I was not going to be a graphic designer. I had no idea what I was going to be. I took it because the letterforms were beautiful.
2. Ten years later I was designing the first Macintosh. We were deciding what fonts the computer should have. Every computer at that time had one font — a monospaced, blocky, ugly font that looked like a typewriter. The question on the table was whether we should put the engineering effort into making the Mac support multiple proportional fonts, with real typography.
3. Because I had taken the calligraphy class — because I knew the difference between a serif and a sans-serif, because I knew what kerning was, because I had spent hours just looking at well-formed letters — I understood at a gut level what was at stake. I said yes, we do the proportional fonts. It became a defining feature of the Mac, and every personal computer after it inherited real typography because the Mac demanded it.
4. Here's the thing. In 1972, there was no way to look at a calligraphy class and say "this will pay off ten years from now in a computer I haven't imagined yet." The computer didn't exist. The connection between the calligraphy and the Mac was not visible looking forward. It only became visible looking backward, once the Mac needed it.
5. So the question you face — which every honest young person faces — is: when I feel a pull toward something that does not fit my career plan, do I trust the pull? The strategic voice says no, follow the plan, do the useful thing. The honest voice says yes, follow the pull, the plan is made up anyway. I'm here to tell you the honest voice is usually right. Not always, but usually. And I'll tell you why.
6. The plan you have at 20 is a plan made by a 20-year-old based on a world that existed when they were 15. The actual world you will live in at 30 is different from both. Your career will not follow the plan. The pulls you feel are information — they are your future self reaching back and telling you where to aim. Follow the pulls even when you cannot justify them. The justifications come later, when the dots connect, and the connections could never have been seen from where you were standing at the beginning.

## Conclusion
Calligraphy paid off ten years later, when the Mac needed typography. The calligraphy class is not the lesson — the lesson is that I could not have known the class would pay off. I took it because I wanted to. The payoff was invisible from 1972. It was only visible from 1984, looking backward. You cannot connect the dots looking forward. You have to trust that the dots will connect later, if the pulls are honest. Trust your gut, your destiny, your life, your karma — whatever word you want to use. The belief that the dots connect is not a guarantee. It's a bet. But it's a bet with a much better payoff than the alternative, which is to only do things whose usefulness you can justify up front. The justifiable things are usually the things everyone is doing. The honest pulls are where the distinctive contributions come from. If you want to make something distinctive, follow the pulls. The dots connect later.

## Abstract Form
Decisions about what to work on are routinely evaluated against a model of the future — a career plan, a strategic roadmap, an expected payoff. This evaluation systematically rejects pulls that do not fit the model, even when the pull itself is honest and deep. The problem is that the model is almost always wrong about the future, and the pulls are information the model cannot incorporate, because the pulls come from parts of the self that know things the rational model does not. The value of a seemingly-useless interest is that it can turn out, ten or twenty years later, to be the exact material some future project needs — and nobody at the present moment can predict which interests will turn out to matter. The strategic calculus that filters out the "useless" interests is therefore filtering out the accidental raw material that would have made a career distinctive. Key signals you are filtering out honest pulls: every project choice you make is justified by a forward-facing argument; you have not deliberately pursued anything "irrelevant" in years; you cannot recall the last time you did something purely because you were drawn to it. Diagnostic question: "What are the pulls I feel that I have been suppressing because I cannot justify them to my career plan? What would happen if I gave one of them a serious amount of time?" The answer is usually: in the short term, nothing. In the long term, sometimes, one of those pulls becomes the dot that connects to something decades later — and nothing about the long-term payoff was predictable at the moment you followed the pull. Follow the pulls even when they look useless. The dots connect later, looking backward. You are not a rational optimizer of your future. You are a person accumulating the raw material for futures you cannot yet imagine.
