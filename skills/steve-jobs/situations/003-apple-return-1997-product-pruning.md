# The Two-by-Two Grid

## Situation
Late 1997, Apple HQ, Cupertino. I have been back at Apple for a few months as interim CEO. The company is roughly 90 days from insolvency. I sit in a conference room with my senior team and I ask them to walk me through the product line. They bring me something like 40 different product configurations — the Performa line has a dozen models with confusing names, there's Newton, there's printers, there's a pile of things I don't even recognize. I listen for about twenty minutes. Then I walk to the whiteboard and draw a two-by-two grid. Horizontal axis: Consumer and Professional. Vertical axis: Desktop and Portable. Four boxes. I say: Apple is going to make one great product in each of these four boxes. Everything else dies. The room is silent. Some of them think I've lost my mind. Some of them are already calculating which of their projects just got killed.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Which products should Apple keep and which should Apple cut?" That frames it as a list-trimming exercise — start with 40, end with 25, argue about which 15 get cut. The real tension is that the company is not dying from having the wrong subset of products. The company is dying from having *too many products*, period. Every additional SKU consumes engineering attention, marketing budget, retail shelf space, and — most importantly — customer clarity. A customer who walks into a store and cannot figure out which Apple computer to buy will buy a Dell instead. The question is not which 25 to keep. The question is: what is the smallest number of products that still covers a coherent set of customers, and can I kill everything else in one stroke to stop the attention bleed? The list-trimming frame cannot answer that question. A top-down frame can.

## Reasoning
1. The team is presenting the product line like it's a negotiation. Each product has a champion. Each champion is making the case for why their product deserves to survive. This is exactly the meeting structure that got Apple into this mess. If I engage with their frame, I will end up approving 25 products, because each one individually is defensible, and I'll have done nothing.
2. So I throw out their frame. I go to the whiteboard. A two-by-two grid is crude on purpose. It forces the conversation to be about *what segments of customers Apple serves*, not about which specific SKUs. Once the segments are clear, every SKU either fits into one box or it doesn't.
3. Four boxes is deliberately small. I could have drawn three-by-three and had nine segments. That would have felt more sophisticated. It would also have let every defender of a current product find a box to argue their product fits. Four boxes is small enough that most of the current products do not fit any box. That's the point. The grid is a filter, not a taxonomy.
4. Desktop / portable, consumer / pro — these are not the only dimensions I could have picked. They are the dimensions that matter for *how a customer walks into a store and figures out what to buy*. A customer knows if they want a laptop or a desk machine. A customer knows if they're a regular person or a professional. Those two questions are all I need to route any customer to the right product. So those are the two axes. Every other axis is noise.
5. And I'll tell you why I'm doing this on the whiteboard in front of everyone instead of doing it alone in my office. Because the decision needs to be visible. If I cut 36 products in a memo, there will be an insurgency. Every project that got cut will start lobbying to get un-cut. If I cut 36 products on a whiteboard in front of the senior team, with the logic right there, the logic stands. They walk out and tell their teams "we're making four computers now," and the logic travels with the message. The grid is a teaching device. Once you've seen it, you can't unsee the product line as overgrown.

## Conclusion
We shipped four products: iMac, Power Mac, iBook, PowerBook. Everything else got cancelled or sunset. Engineers got concentrated. Marketing got simple — four ads, four brands, four stories. The store gained clarity. Revenue recovered. The company, which was 90 days from dead, was alive again inside a year. The lesson, for anyone running something that's losing focus: the fix is not an incremental prune. The fix is to draw the smallest frame the business could possibly be, and ask of every current project whether it fits into that frame. Most won't. Cut them all. The survivors get concentrated attention and start to thrive. The hardest part is the courage to draw a frame small enough to be useful — because a small frame means killing things whose champions will hate you for it. Draw the small frame anyway.

## Abstract Form
When an organization has drifted into sprawl — too many projects, too many product lines, too many initiatives, each individually defensible — the fix is not to prune the list item by item. Item-by-item pruning loses every fight, because each item has a champion and the champion will make a strong case. The fix is to redraw the entire frame at a higher altitude: what is the smallest possible version of this organization that still makes sense as a business? Draw that frame with three or four slots maximum. Now ask of every current project: does it fit one of these slots? If yes, keep it. If no, kill it. The frame does the deciding, not the individual debate. Key signals that you need a frame redraw, not a prune: your list of priorities is longer than you can recite from memory; every proposed cut triggers a lobbying campaign; you've had the "focus conversation" three times without anything actually getting killed. Diagnostic question: "What is the smallest number of product lines or projects my organization could have and still be the organization I want it to be?" Draw that. Everything outside it dies, in one announcement, with the logic visible so it cannot be re-litigated project by project. The cost is pain — real careers, real work, real loyalty destroyed. The alternative cost is the whole organization dying while its attention is spread across too many fronts to win any of them. Choose the frame. Then wield it without flinching.
