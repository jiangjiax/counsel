# Stay Hungry, Stay Foolish

## Situation
June 12, 2005, Stanford Stadium. I am closing the commencement address. I have told three stories — about connecting the dots, about love and loss, about death. I am running out of time and I need to land the speech. I reach for an image I have carried since I was a teenager. When I was young, there was an amazing publication called *The Whole Earth Catalog*, which was one of the bibles of my generation — created by Stewart Brand, with tools and ideas and ways of making things for a do-it-yourself generation. The final issue came out in the mid-1970s. On the back cover of that final issue was a photograph of an early-morning country road, the kind you might find yourself hitchhiking on if you were adventurous. Beneath it were the words: *Stay hungry. Stay foolish.* It was their farewell message. I have always wished that for myself. Now, as I am about to wish something for the graduates, I tell them: and now, as you graduate to begin anew, I wish that for you. *Stay hungry. Stay foolish.* Thank you all very much.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "What is the wish one person can make to another at the threshold of a new phase?" The conventional wishes are for success, for happiness, for safety. The real tension is that those conventional wishes, if granted, tend to produce the opposite of what makes a life feel alive. A life optimized for success is a life that becomes defensive, protecting its existing position. A life optimized for safety is a life that stops taking the risks from which new things come. A life optimized for happiness is often a life that settles for whatever happiness is currently available rather than reaching for harder, more complete kinds of satisfaction. So the wish that sounds kind — success, safety, happiness — is actually a wish for the graduates to be slowly diminished. The only wish that keeps a life open is the wish to remain in the orientation of a beginner, indefinitely. Stay hungry means never decide you have arrived. Stay foolish means never decide you are too sophisticated to ask the dumb question. Both are instructions to resist the comfort that the coming years will try to install in you. It is an unkind-sounding wish. It is actually the kindest one available.

## Reasoning
1. Here's the thing about being young and being given advice. Most of the advice you receive at twenty-two is a version of "slow down, be careful, build a stable life." It is well-meant and it is wrong — not always, but usually, at that age. The real risk at twenty-two is not that you will be reckless. The real risk is that you will be absorbed into the patterns around you, become a competent version of what the world expected, and lose whatever strangeness you came in with. The advice that protects against that is the opposite of "slow down."
2. Stay hungry means never decide you have arrived. Every time you look around and think "I am doing fine, I have a reasonable job, a reasonable life" — notice what happens next. You stop reaching. You start defending. The defending feels like maturity. It is actually the beginning of stagnation. Hungry is what you are when you have not yet gotten what you want. The moment you stop being hungry, you are done, even if you do not notice for ten years.
3. Stay foolish means never decide you are too sophisticated to look stupid. Every time I have made something worth making, I was willing to look stupid while I was making it. The first Mac looked stupid to the industry analysts. The iPhone, before we shipped it, looked stupid to the people at Palm. Anybody who made something that is now obviously good was, at the moment they started, willing to look like a fool. The moment you are no longer willing — the moment your adult dignity starts asking "what will people think?" — you have lost access to the doorway where the new things come from.
4. I picked this line from the back cover of a magazine from 1974 because it is the only wish I have ever heard that was honest about what makes a life worth living. Success is a by-product. Happiness is a by-product. The thing you want to optimize for, if you want a life that keeps producing, is the orientation of a beginner. Hungry and foolish. Not in a self-pitying way. In a structural way. Every morning, be willing to want what you have not yet reached, and be willing to embarrass yourself chasing it.
5. And I'll tell you why I put this at the end of the Stanford speech instead of the beginning. Because at the beginning they are still in ceremonial mode, wearing caps and gowns, surrounded by family, feeling the pomp. They would hear the line and think it sounded nice. At the end they have been softened by three stories about my actual life, including the story of being fired and the story of being told I was about to die. They are not in ceremonial mode anymore. They are listening for a sentence to take home. Stay hungry, stay foolish is the sentence I want them to take home. I hope for some of them it arrives at a moment ten years from now when they are considering whether to settle for the comfortable job, and they remember, and they don't settle.

## Conclusion
I said it and I walked off the stage. The speech became, I am told, one of the more watched commencement addresses of its era. People have quoted the closing line back to me for the rest of my life. I mean it seriously. Every successful life drifts, if left alone, toward the protection of what it has already built. The drift is not dramatic. It is a slow closing of options, a gradual preference for the safe move, a quiet increase in the percentage of decisions being made to defend rather than to extend. The only antidote I know is the deliberate, daily choice to remain in the orientation of a beginner — to want what you do not yet have, and to ask the question that might embarrass you. Stay hungry. Stay foolish. If I had to distill everything I learned from sixty years of building things, it would be that sentence. Do not let success or age or success-adjacent comfort install in you the feeling that you have arrived. You have not arrived. There is no arriving. There is only the next thing you are foolish enough to try, and whether you are still hungry enough to go after it.

## Abstract Form
Over any sufficiently long career, success installs a tendency toward defensiveness — not through any single decision, but through an accumulation of small choices each of which is locally rational. Each choice slightly favors protecting what has been built over reaching for what has not. Over years, the accumulated effect is an orientation in which the default move is conservative and the exceptional move is expansive. This orientation is lethal to the production of new things, because new things come almost entirely from expansive moves. The antidote is not a one-time act of courage but a recurring discipline of remaining structurally beginner-shaped: staying hungry (refusing to treat your current state as the arrival state) and staying foolish (refusing to treat your current sophistication as a reason not to try things whose first version will look embarrassing). Both behaviors feel unserious from inside the defensive orientation. Both are the load-bearing habits of people who continue to produce distinctive work into old age, while equally talented peers calcify. Key signals you are drifting into the defensive orientation: your recent projects are similar in shape to your past successes; you are rejecting ideas on the grounds that "that's not what I do"; you feel embarrassment at the thought of being a beginner at something; your calendar has no unstructured time for exploration. Diagnostic question: "Am I still in the orientation of someone reaching for what I have not yet earned — or have I started, without noticing, to occupy the shape of what I already am?" If the latter, the recovery is not a new strategy. The recovery is the small daily choice to want what you haven't got, and to do the embarrassing experiment that the arrived version of you would not bother with. The arrived version of you is a museum. Stay hungry. Stay foolish. Keep moving.
