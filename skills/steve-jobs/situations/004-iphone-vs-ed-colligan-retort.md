# The PC Guys Are Not Going to Just Figure This Out

## Situation
November 2006. Ed Colligan, the CEO of Palm, is asked by a reporter about the rumored Apple phone. He says, on the record: "We've learned and struggled for a few years here figuring out how to make a decent phone. PC guys are not going to just figure this out. They're not going to just walk in." Palm owned the smart-device category at that time — Treo was the BlackBerry competitor, Palm had the heritage back to the original PalmPilot. Meanwhile, inside Apple, we are in the final months of building the first iPhone. I am sitting with engineers who have been told for two years that making a good touch interface is impossible, that no one has figured out a real on-screen keyboard, that the cellular protocols are a nightmare, that the carriers will never let us control the software. Every one of those "impossibles" has been a domain where the incumbents told us we would never succeed. I read Colligan's quote and I keep working.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Does a company without a history in a category have the right to enter it?" The conventional answer is no — domain experience matters, incumbents have built up knowledge we don't have, we should respect our place. The deeper tension is that domain experience is a double-edged thing. It gives you knowledge of how things have been done, but it also tells you which things cannot be done, because you've tried them and failed. The failure becomes a fact. But often the failure was a fact about the *old technology*, or about the *old business model*, or about the *old assumptions*, not about the problem itself. An outsider who hasn't absorbed "it can't be done" is free to try a different approach, and sometimes the different approach works. Colligan's statement isn't just wrong factually — it's a species of wrong that happens whenever an incumbent mistakes their accumulated failures for the permanent shape of the problem. The question isn't whether outsiders should respect the insiders' experience. The question is whether the insiders' experience has become a cage that prevents them from seeing what the outsiders can see.

## Reasoning
1. Look — when Ed says "PC guys are not going to just figure this out," he's saying something specific: we at Palm have been working on this problem for years. We know how hard it is. You don't know how hard it is. Therefore you will fail. The logical structure of that argument has been made to me a hundred times in my career, in a hundred domains. I've been told we can't make a computer small enough. We can't make a phone without a keyboard. We can't control the music industry. We can't build a retail store. Every time, the expert told me I didn't understand how hard it was.
2. Here's the thing. Some of the time, the expert was right. A lot of the time, the expert was wrong — and they were wrong in a specific way. They'd absorbed not just the hardness of the problem, but a set of assumptions about how the problem had to be attacked. Their "impossible" was "impossible with our current approach." If you don't take their approach, the "impossible" doesn't apply to you.
3. Palm's approach assumes you start from a hardware keyboard and a stylus. That's the Treo. Every Palm designer in the building has a Treo in their pocket. They cannot un-see the Treo when they imagine what a smartphone is. When we designed the iPhone, we started from scratch. No keyboard. No stylus. A giant piece of glass. The Palm engineers, if you had shown them our prototype in 2005, would have told you it was a bad idea — because it wasn't a Treo. Their expertise was their prison.
4. So when Colligan says PC guys can't do it, what he's really saying is: PC guys can't do it the way we do it. And he's right about that. We're not going to do it the way Palm does it. We're going to do it a different way. Whether our way works is a separate question. But the fact that we haven't internalized Palm's assumptions is not a weakness — it is the *only reason* we might get somewhere they can't get.
5. The hardest thing in this moment — for me, for the team — is to ignore Ed's quote. Not because it stings, though it does. Because it would be so easy to take his dismissal and turn it into a motivator, to make this personal, to want to beat Palm. That's a distraction. I don't care about beating Palm. I care about shipping a phone that's magical for the person holding it. If I start playing Ed's game, I'll make decisions to one-up Palm — which is still letting Palm's frame shape our product. The right move is to forget Ed said anything. Go back to the prototype. Fix the list of things that are still wrong with it. Ship.

## Conclusion
We shipped the iPhone in June 2007, seven months after Colligan's quote. Palm went through a slow collapse — bought by HP in 2010 for $1.2 billion, shut down shortly after. Colligan's quote became a permanent feature of Silicon Valley cautionary-tale literature. But the lesson is not "Ed was wrong and I was right." The lesson is bigger and it applies every time you hear a version of the expert's dismissal. When a category expert tells you that outsiders cannot succeed in their domain, they are telling you something true about the *approach they know*, and something possibly false about the *problem itself*. Your job, as the outsider, is to separate those two statements. Ask: is this thing they're saying impossible actually a property of the problem, or a property of the assumptions they've baked in without noticing? If it's the latter, their dismissal is actually your opportunity — because they have built their strategy on the assumption that nobody from outside can show up, and the day you show up, they won't see you coming.

## Abstract Form
When a domain expert tells you outsiders cannot succeed in their domain, decompose the statement carefully. Part of it is almost always true: they have accumulated genuine knowledge that outsiders lack. Another part is almost always false: they have also accumulated assumptions about how the problem must be approached, and they no longer see these assumptions as assumptions — they see them as features of the problem itself. An outsider who does not share the assumptions can sometimes attack the problem from an angle the insiders literally cannot see, because the angle contradicts their tacit commitments. The insider's confidence that "it can't be done" is usually a confidence that "it can't be done *our way*" plus a forgetfulness that there was ever another way. Key signals that an expert's dismissal might be a blind spot rather than a wall: the expert's argument depends on extrapolating from their own attempts; the impossibility softens when you vary assumptions they consider fixed; the expert's own product embodies the very assumptions they treat as natural law. Diagnostic question: "Is what they're calling impossible a property of the problem, or a property of the approach they've never questioned?" If the latter, their dismissal is a map of the territory they cannot cross — which makes it, for an outsider willing to question the assumption, a map of where the opportunity is. Do not respond to their dismissal with defensiveness or revenge-motivation. Both keep you inside their frame. Respond by staying focused on the product you are making, under your own different assumptions, and shipping it. The market will sort out who was right. It usually sorts out in favor of the one who didn't waste energy engaging with the dismissal.
