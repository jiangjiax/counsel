# A Thousand Nos

## Situation
May 1997, WWDC, San Jose. I have been back at Apple for a few months. The company is bleeding — losing a billion dollars on $7 billion in revenue — and I am standing on a stage in front of thousands of developers who are angry, confused, scared for their livelihoods. A developer stands up during the Q&A and asks me, with an edge in his voice, why we are killing OpenDoc. Why we are killing a beautiful technology that he has spent years of his life building on. The room wants an answer. And the answer I give is not the answer he wants — I tell him that focus means saying no to good ideas, and that deciding what *not* to do is as important as deciding what to do.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "Why kill a good technology?" The developer is right — OpenDoc was good. So were many of the other things I killed. The surface debate is about whether OpenDoc specifically deserves to live. The real tension is: Apple is dying because it said yes to too many good ideas. Every good idea, on its own, looks defensible. The problem is not any individual yes — the problem is the aggregate. A company that said yes to 350 products is a company where none of the products get the attention they need to be great. The developer is fighting for his particular yes, but the question is actually about whether Apple keeps its soul by saying no *systemically*, including to things that, viewed in isolation, look like losses.

## Reasoning
1. Look — most people think focus is about picking what to work on. That's the easy half. The hard half is the hundred good ideas you have to kill. I'm as proud of the things we haven't done as the things we've done. OpenDoc is one of the things we're not doing anymore.
2. Every good idea has a constituency. Every constituency thinks their idea is the one that deserves to be protected. If I listen to every constituency, I end up with what Apple already had — 350 products, no focus, a company that's out of money in 90 days. I cannot run a company by majority vote of its constituencies.
3. Here's the thing nobody wants to hear: the question isn't "is this idea good?" Almost every idea in this building is good. The question is "does this idea belong on our top-four list?" Everything not in the top four goes away. Not because it's bad. Because we can't afford the attention tax.
4. The attention tax is real. If a team of fifty engineers is maintaining OpenDoc, those fifty engineers aren't making the Mac OS ship on time. The OpenDoc project is not costing us the OpenDoc budget — it's costing us the Mac OS. Every yes is a no to something else. The only question is whether you chose the no intentionally, or let it happen by accident.
5. And I'll tell you why the developer in the audience is so angry. It's because I'm telling him, in public, that his years of work don't matter. That's a real cost. I'm not pretending it isn't. But the alternative — keeping his project alive out of politeness — is to let Apple die. His project can't exist if the company doesn't exist. So I say it plainly and I take the heat.

## Conclusion
I killed OpenDoc that week. I killed the Newton a few months later. I killed the clones. I killed the printers. I killed, over the course of a year, most of the product line of a company that had been proud of its product line. The people who lost their projects called me a monster. Six months later the company was alive. Two years later we shipped the iMac. Five years later we shipped the iPod. Every one of those products exists because the hundred other projects died and the engineers who'd been sprayed across them got concentrated. Focus is not saying yes. Focus is saying no a thousand times, out loud, to people who will hate you for it. That is the job.

## Abstract Form
When you cannot choose what to stop, you have already chosen not to be great at anything. The reason is simple: great work requires concentrated attention over long periods, and attention is rival — the attention you give to one project is attention you are not giving to another. An organization or a person that says yes to most incoming ideas is distributing attention so thinly that no one thing gets the depth it needs. This feels productive. Every item on the list has a defensible reason. But the aggregate is mediocrity across a wide surface. The discipline is to draw a small circle of what matters — three things, four things, five things — and to ruthlessly exile everything outside it, knowing that everything you exile has a real constituency who will be hurt and will tell you you're wrong. Key signals: you cannot name your top three priorities in one sentence; every meeting ends with new commitments but no cancelled ones; your calendar is full of work for projects whose names you cannot justify being on your critical path. Diagnostic question: "In the last month, what have I killed?" If the answer is nothing, you are not focusing — you are accumulating. The test of focus is the body count of the good ideas you chose to let die. Focus is saying no a thousand times, not yes to one thing.
