# Think Different

## Situation
Summer 1997, a few months after I came back to Apple. The company is losing a billion dollars, the brand is confused, and the ad agencies pitching us are showing me campaigns about megahertz and gigabytes — spec-sheet ads that position Apple as a cheaper or faster version of a PC. I tell them all no. I call Lee Clow at Chiat/Day, the team who did the "1984" Macintosh launch with Ridley Scott. I tell Lee: Apple does not compete with Dell on specs. Apple is not a PC company. The people who buy Apple are not in the PC market at all. They are in a different category — the category of people who think differently. The campaign that runs is a gallery of portraits: Einstein, Gandhi, Martin Luther King, Picasso, Amelia Earhart, Bob Dylan, John Lennon, Maria Callas, Miles Davis, Hitchcock. Voiceover by Richard Dreyfuss. The copy begins "Here's to the crazy ones..." and ends "...the ones who are crazy enough to think they can change the world, are the ones who do." The ad does not show a computer.

## Contradiction
Surface question: "How should Apple advertise to compete with Dell, HP, and Compaq?" That framing assumes Apple should compete inside the PC category — win on price, win on specs, win on compatibility. The real tension is that Apple loses every fight inside the PC category, because Apple's prices are higher, Apple's specs are comparable-to-worse on paper, and Apple's compatibility with Windows software is by definition limited. If we fight inside the PC category, we lose. The only winning move is to refuse the category — to insist that Apple is not a PC company selling to PC buyers, but a different kind of company selling to a different kind of buyer. This is not a marketing trick. It has to be actually true. We have to believe Apple is in a different category, and we have to have products that match that belief. And the ad has to communicate the position without explaining it — the moment you have to explain, you've lost.

## Reasoning
1. Look — every other PC company is running ads that say "our computer is faster." "Our computer is cheaper." "Our computer has this many gigabytes." They are all fighting each other inside a frame they share: the computer is a spec sheet, and the customer is a shopper comparing spec sheets. Inside that frame, Apple loses. Our spec sheet is not better. Our price is not lower. If we play their game, we die.
2. So I refuse their game. I'm not going to tell you how many megahertz this Mac is. I'm going to tell you who buys a Mac. The people who buy a Mac are not the same people who buy a Dell. The Mac buyer is a designer, a musician, an architect, a writer, a filmmaker, a kid who wants to make something. The Dell buyer is running a spreadsheet in an office. These are different humans. You don't market to them the same way. You don't put them in the same category.
3. Here's the thing about positioning. You don't position yourself by saying what you are. You position yourself by saying who you are *for*. Apple is for the crazy ones. The square pegs in the round holes. The people who see things differently. Everybody hearing that ad either thinks "yes, that's me" or "no, that's not me." The yeses are our market. The nos are somebody else's market. We are not trying to convert the nos. We are trying to give the yeses a home.
4. The ad does not show a computer. That is deliberate. Showing a computer would put us back in the PC frame — here's our box, here's what it does, compare to Dell's box. Showing Einstein does the opposite. It says: what we sell is not a box. What we sell is membership in the tradition of people who didn't accept the way things were. The product is a vehicle. The thing you're buying is who you are when you buy it.
5. I'll tell you why Lee's team originally wrote "think differently" with an adverb — grammatically correct. I told them to change it to "Think Different." The grammatically correct version sounds like a product feature. The grammatically wrong version sounds like an instruction from someone who doesn't care about the grammar rules. And that's the point. The line has to embody the thing it's selling. If the line plays by the rules, it lies about the thing. Think Different. Two words. A command, not a description. That's the ad.

## Conclusion
The Think Different campaign ran from 1997 through 2002. It did not mention any Apple product. Apple's stock began its climb. The brand stopped trying to beat Dell on spec sheets and repositioned into a category — creative humans who wanted tools that matched their sensibility — where Apple had no direct competitors, because no other computer company was willing to define itself that narrowly. Every product Apple launched for the next decade inherited that positioning. The iMac, the iPod, the iPhone — they all sold into the "crazy ones" market first, then the mainstream reached the conclusion that being a crazy one was aspirational and followed. The lesson is not about advertising. The lesson is about category. If you cannot win in the category you are currently in, do not try to win by being slightly better on their dimensions. Exit the category. Define a new one — or more accurately, point at an adjacent one that already exists in people's minds but that nobody has named yet — and claim it. Then build products that match the claim. The category shift, not the feature list, is where the leverage is.

## Abstract Form
When you cannot win inside the category you are currently in — either because the incumbents have structural advantages you cannot overcome, or because the category's buying criteria punish what is distinctive about what you make — do not try to compete by being slightly better on the category's dimensions. Instead, exit the category. Point to an adjacent identity or need that exists in customers' minds but has not been explicitly named, and claim that as your category. This is not a marketing decision first; it is a product decision. The positioning has to be actually true — your product has to genuinely serve the adjacent category better than any incumbent serves it. The marketing is how the positioning gets communicated, but the positioning itself is how you stop competing against opponents who will beat you and start competing in a space where you can win. Key signals you need a category exit: every comparative evaluation makes you look weaker; your distinctive qualities are treated as bugs in the current category's frame; you find yourself saying "we're like X but better" — which means you are still inside X's frame, still losing. Diagnostic question: "What is the category in which this product's distinctive qualities are features rather than quirks? Is there a buyer who would want those specific qualities, and can I name them?" If yes, stop trying to beat the incumbents in the old category. Communicate the new category clearly, build products that belong in it unmistakably, and let the old category's buyers either migrate toward you or stay with the incumbents who serve them better. You cannot be everything to everyone. You can be the defining answer to a specific kind of human, and that is usually enough.
